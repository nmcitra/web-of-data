The Candlemaker’s Blindness
On the Manufacture of Darkness, with Observations on the Bee

In March, before the desert blooms, a scout returns to the hive.

She has found something — a stand of globe mallow along the arroyo, half a mile south, orange bowl blossoms open to the sun. She begins to dance. The waggle dance lasts seconds to minutes. It costs her real energy — metabolic resources she cannot recover. She encodes distance, direction, and quality in the movement of her body. The other foragers watch. They evaluate. The signal is honest because it is expensive. A bee cannot fake the dance. The cost is the proof.

The colony does not vote on whether to trust her. The colony does not check her credentials. The colony watches her spend herself, and the spending is the information.

In March, before spring break, a woman walks into a government office holding her children’s birth certificates and a letter that says she owes money she doesn’t owe.

The system that sent the letter has no face, no phone number, no office she can visit. She will spend her morning in a plastic chair, holding a number, waiting for someone to tell her what happened. The person who designed the system is in a conference room three states away, looking at a dashboard that says the error rate is acceptable.

The scout spent herself and the hive responded. The woman will spend her morning and no one will respond. The signal she carries — *this is wrong, I can prove it, here are the birth certificates* — is costly. She took the bus. She left work. She brought the documents. The system does not read costly signals. It reads data fields.

The hive makes comb.

Not for the candlemaker. The hive has never heard of a candlemaker. The comb is architecture — hexagonal cells at precisely the angle that maximizes strength per unit of wax. It is temperature-regulated: the cluster maintains 35°C year-round through collective metabolic labor. It is information infrastructure: each cell stores either honey, pollen, or brood, and the colony knows which is where because the bees who built it are the bees who use it.

The comb is not a product. It is a commons. Every bee who deposits nectar into a cell is trusting that other bees will convert it to honey, cap it, and leave it for winter. Every bee who feeds a larva is trusting that the larva will emerge and join the labor. The reciprocity is not enforced by contract. It is structured by proximity — you do the work because you are in the comb, and being in the comb is the signal.

No bee is anonymous. No bee works remotely. Every bee who contributes is witnessed by the bees she works beside. There are no agentic bees.

Every institution humans have built in the last three hundred years is a buffer between the individual and the environment. The university, the corporation, the platform, the credential system. Buffers are useful. You don’t want your surgeon worrying about rent during your operation.

But the buffer has a cost. And the cost is invisible from inside it.

When Kant wrote sentences that required three readings to parse, the university paid for that. His salary didn’t depend on being understood. The institution carried the signal even when the signal was too weak to carry itself. He couldn’t see that because he didn’t have to.

When Jefferson wrote “all men are created equal” while owning human beings, the institution of American governance paid for that. His political authority didn’t depend on consistency.

Olympe de Gouges ran the consistency audit Jefferson never had to. She rewrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man as the Declaration of the Rights of Woman, word for word, holding the mirror so close to the original that the inconsistency became impossible to ignore.

The institution’s response was not revision. It was the guillotine.

> Olympe de Gouges was executed by guillotine in Paris on 3 November 1793, condemned by the Revolutionary Tribunal during the Reign of Terror for seditious political writings and alleged attempts to restore monarchy or undermine the one and indivisible republic.

De Gouges was a bee. She did the dance — the costly, embodied, witnessed dance. She spent herself. The institution melted her down for wax.

The candlemaker arrives in autumn.

He does not tend the hive. He does not know the scout’s dance or the nurse bee’s schedule or the architect’s geometry. He does not maintain the temperature. He does not participate in the reciprocity that produced the comb over thousands of hours of collective labor.

The first thing the candlemaker does is board up the windows.

Not out of malice. Out of business. Candles sell better in the dark. A room full of daylight has no use for his product. So he builds a room without daylight — and then he opens the hive, takes the comb, melts it down, and sells the light.

The light is beautiful. The rooms it illuminates are warm. The people in those rooms read books, write declarations, build philosophies. They do not think about the hive. They do not think about the windows. Why would they? The candle is right there. The light is right there.

They do not notice that the sun is also right there — on the other side of the boards.

The candlemaker’s blindness is not ignorance. It is the structural consequence of manufacturing the darkness that makes your product necessary. He cannot see that the light is borrowed because he’s the one who sealed out the free version. He has never danced. He has never spent himself on a signal that costs real energy to produce. He has never opened a window. He has only ever sold the output.

Five things the candlemaker cannot see. They’re all the same thing from different angles.

**The cost of the wax.** Emilie du Châtelet translated Newton and added original derivations. No institution vouched for her. She built a laboratory at Cirey with her own money. Phillis Wheatley wrote a poem so technically accomplished that a panel of eighteen men had to convene to verify a Black woman could have written it. Mary Wollstonecraft wrote *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* while poor and unprotected, knowing it would cost her what little standing she had. Each one paid the full metabolic cost of credibility — the dance the institution waives for those it recognizes. When credentials carry your credibility, you don’t know what credibility weighs. The hive spent thousands of hours producing the comb. The candlemaker spent an afternoon melting it.

**The architecture he’s destroying.** The Golden Cue sat on 4th and Manuel in Albuquerque. All ages, all the time, no alcohol — just twenty full-size tables, basic food, and a man named Red who called everyone “guy.” Hey guy, you want this or that? His voice was grumbly and he drove a blue Pontiac with faded paint. Red knew who came in after school and who came in after work. He knew who was good for a table and who needed to be spotted. The Golden Cue was comb — information architecture maintained through daily embodied encounter, held together by a man who never used anyone’s name and remembered everything about them. Whatever replaced it was cheaper or newer or nothing at all. It was also a room with the windows boarded up. The acequia that fed the North Valley is being paved for development. The library where the after-school kids did homework is losing its funding. The porch where neighbors talked is replaced by a feed where strangers perform. Each one is a window sealed. Each one creates a market for the candle that replaces what was free. The comb is gone. The candlemaker wonders why there’s no more wax.

**The hive he depends on.** A close family friend is finishing her degree at NMSU while teaching full-time at Zia Elementary in Las Cruces. She buys supplies with her own money. She arrives before the building is warm. She knows which kids didn’t eat breakfast. She is the comb — the trust infrastructure between the institution and the child. My daughter’s school needed props for a play. They sent a broadcast message asking for fifteen flashlights and a few other things. The parents stepped up. The teachers pitched in. No contract. No platform. Just the colony reading the signal and responding, the way colonies do — because proximity is the mechanism and reciprocity is the architecture. When we say “the schools are failing,” we mean the candlemaker took the comb and the colony is rebuilding in real time with flashlights and out-of-pocket money and six-thirty mornings. The colony will rebuild. Bees always do — if there are enough of them, if the queen survives, if the environment provides. But every time the comb is taken, the colony starts over. The accumulated architecture is gone. The new comb works. It is not the old comb. The colony rebuilds. It is never as strong.

**The consensus he never witnesses.** September 19, 2025. The Doña Ana County Commission chamber has metal detectors at the entrance, which is new. The room is packed. The agenda has one item that matters: a $165 billion industrial revenue bond for Project Jupiter, a data center campus in Santa Teresa that would be the largest private investment in New Mexico history. The developers promised twice-weekly community meetings. Five happened. People hold signs that read *You can’t drink data*. For seven hours, residents stand and speak. They talk about water. They talk about the aquifer. They talk about what happens when you trade thirty years of property tax revenue for a promise. Commissioner Susana Chaparro moves to postpone the vote. The motion dies without a second. She moves again. It dies again. She moves a third time. The room watches a woman do the dance — the costly, embodied, witnessed dance — and the hive does not read it. The vote is 4–1. The room erupts. People shout *sellouts*. Then they begin to chant: *this is what democracy looks like*. The acequia commission meets every spring and every parciante has a vote. The town hall used to work the same way. The candlemaker doesn’t see the consensus because he was never in the room when it happened. In this case, he was in the room. He just wasn’t listening.

**The winter that’s coming.** The colony stores honey for winter. The comb is the granary. When the candlemaker takes the comb in autumn, he takes the winter reserves. A seventy-year-old parciante on the acequia can tell you what happens when the ditch isn’t cleaned — not from a report, from memory. The water didn’t come. The crops died. The family went hungry. She cannot see the winter abstractly because she has lived through the winter concretely. The candlemaker cannot see the winter because he does not live in the hive. His winter is different from theirs. This is the deepest blindness — the inability to see that your extraction has consequences in a season you will never inhabit.

Eleven p.m. on a Tuesday. A fraud analyst is looking at a transaction that the model scored as clean. Something is wrong with it. She can’t name what — the features are normal, the velocity is normal, the amount is normal. But the pattern doesn’t smell right. She flags it. Three days later, the investigation confirms: a synthetic identity, eighteen months in the making, just triggered its first cash-out.

The model didn’t see it. She did. Not because she’s smarter than the model. Because she reads what lives between the features — [the dark matter](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/how-to-build-a-dark-matter-detector-bbdecff5751a) the model was never trained to measure. Every knowledge system has rooms it can see and rooms it can’t. The model lives in the rooms where the data is clean and labeled. She lives in the hallway between rooms, where the signal has no name yet.

A [poet](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/el-mundo-d66e667c95b4) on a sidewalk in Madrid once wrote me a poem in a language I don’t speak. I understood every word. The fraud analyst understands the transaction the same way.

The person at the center of the network sees the average.

The person at the edge sees the variance.

And the variance is where reality leaks through the model.

She’s a scout. She did the dance. The institution should read it.

AI is the candlemaker at industrial scale.

Every capability it adds removes one constraint between you and your environment. Writing for you. Thinking for you. Analyzing for you. Deciding for you. Each one a window sealed. Each sealed window creates a new market for a candle. The machine melts the comb faster than the colony can rebuild it, and manufactures the darkness faster than the colony can find the sun.

Facebook didn’t connect people who couldn’t connect. It replaced the porch, the library, the third place, boarded up the windows of human connection, and sold candles to people who used to get the light for free. The platform is a room with no windows selling illumination at scale.

We are building, with extraordinary speed and enormous investment, the most sophisticated darkness-manufacturing machine in history. A machine that eliminates the costly signals, the embodied encounters, and the consistency audits that the hive depends on, then sells artificial versions of each one back to us.

This is not an argument against AI. It’s a specification for how AI has to be built.

The hive cannot ignore the season. Neither should the algorithm. Autonomy that exceeds environment is a candle pretending to be the sun. De Gouges held the mirror. She was the colony’s immune system. We keep melting her down and wondering why the infection spreads.

The scout who found the globe mallow, the analyst who flagged the transaction, Red who remembered everyone without learning their names, the teacher who arrives at six-thirty, the commissioner who moved three times, the parciante who remembers when the water didn’t come. They are the variance. They speak the third language.

The system that optimizes them out of the model optimizes itself out of contact with reality.

In March, before the desert blooms, the colony faces a choice it doesn’t know it’s making.

The scout returns with her dance. The foragers watch. The costly signal is read. The comb is built. The honey is stored. The brood is fed. The architecture accumulates, cell by hexagonal cell, through reciprocal labor witnessed by every bee in the hive.

This has happened every spring for a hundred million years.

The candlemaker arrives in autumn. He takes the comb. He sells the light. He does not see the architecture he’s destroying because he has never lived inside it.

The Enlightenment women lived inside it. The acequia operator lives inside it. The fraud analyst at eleven p.m. lives inside it. They can see the comb because they helped build it. They can see the cost because they paid it. They can see the winter because they’ll be the ones who starve.

The light is beautiful. The rooms are warm. The declarations are being written.

But the wax is borrowed. The dance was someone else’s. The windows are boarded. And the spring will come whether the hive survives or not.

Who opens the window?
