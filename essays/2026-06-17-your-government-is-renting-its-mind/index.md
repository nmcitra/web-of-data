Your Government Is Renting Its Mind
On the folly of governing with a mind one does not own

On the night of September 26, 2024, the emergency department at Mission Hospital in Asheville had 100 beds and twice that many patients. Two to a room. Nurses carrying ten patients each, then twelve. The rain had started two days early, a stalled front wringing tropical moisture over the Blue Ridge, so the ground was saturated and the rivers full before Helene’s core even reached the mountains.

A travel nurse two months into her assignment watched a new ambulance arrive every two to five minutes. At the peak she counted four hundred people in the emergency room. By Monday the hospital’s CEO, Greg Lowe, reported more than 900 ER visits and over 200 people admitted for flood injuries, 700 patients in the building, 80 still waiting in the ER for a bed. One nurse described who was arriving by then: people pulled from the river, people who had army-crawled out of the mountains after watching their houses wash away.

This is what a system looks like when demand outruns the capacity to meet it. There is a name for the line a hospital crosses here. Mission moved into crisis protocol, the state where you stop giving each patient the standard of care and start triaging the population, because there is no longer enough of you to go around.

The storm did not only send patients. At the same moment, from the other direction, it took away the hospital’s ability to treat them.

Mission lost commercial power and running water on the first day. The sewage backed up; the toilets would not flush. The plant that pipes water down from the mountains had been destroyed. Nurses flushed toilets by hand with five-gallon buckets, one counting eighty in a single shift. To decontaminate patients without working showers, they poured water from clean garbage cans. Surgical instruments had to be driven two hours to Charlotte to be sterilized, so patients waiting on open-heart surgery waited longer, because the power was back but there was no clean water to run sterile processing.

Watch what that does to the math. A surge raises the number of people who need care. A severed lifeline lowers the capacity to give it. The storm pushed the top of the fraction up and knocked the bottom out at once. That is not two problems. It is one storm working both sides of a single ratio, and it is why the breaking point arrives faster than anyone plans for.

It also ran on clocks that could not keep time with one another. The storm did its damage in hours. The city’s water did not return for ten weeks, not until December 8. A hospital that waits for a slow lifeline to catch up to a fast disaster dies in the gap between the two.

So what kept Mission running was not a backup in the way we usually mean the word. It was capability the hospital could hold in its own hands:

- **Two wells, drilled on campus in the first days after the storm.** The hospital stopped depending on water that came from somewhere else and started making its own.
- **Tanker trucks delivering more than 200,000 gallons a day,** with engineers flown in to plumb that water straight into the building’s pipes. Fourteen million gallons over ten weeks.

The lesson is not “have a backup.” Everyone has a backup. It is the difference between recovery and operation. Recovery says we will be back in three days. Operation says we never went dark, because the thing we depend on lives inside our walls and not upstream where someone else holds the valve.

Hold that picture, because it describes a building you cannot see.

Governments now hand more and more of the decisions that shape your life to AI systems: your benefits claim, your loan, your visa, the fraud flag on your account. Almost none of those systems own the thing they run on. They rent it. The reasoning your government applies to you is borrowed, and the reason it rents matters, because the easy explanation, that the institutions were careless, is wrong.

They rent because of speed. The most capable models run on a machine clock, rebuilt at the pace of the fastest engineering on earth, each one costing hundreds of millions of dollars. We call it the frontier because it keeps moving. A county office runs on a slower clock entirely: budgets approved once a year, procurement measured in months. Ask that office to build its own frontier model and you are asking the slow clock to outrun the fast one. It cannot, and the gap only widens. Four clocks run through this story: the machine’s milliseconds, a caseworker’s hours, an institution’s months, and the years over which the damage shows. Trouble comes whenever one is forced to keep pace with another. Renting is not negligence. It is the rational surrender of an institution that can never catch a frontier built to outrun it.

So the dependency is structure, not accident, and it lands on the same two-sided ratio as the hospital. The demands on that office, the claims and the fraud and the sheer volume, arrive on the machine clock, fast and rising. The capacity to meet them is rented on someone else’s clock and can be cut on a third party’s. Numerator up, denominator borrowed. And because only a handful of labs can run on the fast clock, almost everyone rents from almost no one. The capability is concentrated, and concentrated capability has a single throat to choke.

On June 12, 2026, someone choked it. The U.S. government issued an export-control directive ordering Anthropic to cut off access to its two most capable models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, to any foreign national, inside or outside the country, including the company’s own foreign employees. Anthropic said the net effect was that it had to “abruptly disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all our customers to ensure compliance.” Reuters, Defense One, and Fortune reported the same sequence. One directive, one company, and the most capable reasoning a great many businesses had built on went dark overnight. The government did not have to reach a thousand institutions. It reached one, and a thousand lost the lights.

Picture who waits at the bottom of that. A woman who filed an unemployment claim sits on the far end of an office whose system can no longer tell a real application from a synthetic one, because the model that drew the line went dark on a Thursday. Her claim is not denied. It gets nothing. No determination, no letter, no person to call: the same silence the patient on the gurney met when the sterile room had power but no water. She is the open-heart case of the administrative state, waiting on a capability the building rented and lost.

This is what traditional disaster planning was never built for. Back up your data and you can restore your records after a fire. But data at rest does not reason. When a directive cuts your provider, your agents do not slow down. They stop thinking. You did not lose your filing cabinet. You lost the part of the building that decides.

It had happened before, with a quieter lever. In February 2025 the United States sanctioned Karim Khan, the chief prosecutor of the International Criminal Court. Within months he had lost his Microsoft email and moved to a Swiss provider; by October the court was migrating off Microsoft Office entirely. Different mechanism, identical result: a capability you believed was yours, switched off from upstream, because it was never yours to begin with.

You do not have a capability. You have a borrowed one. A generator with no fuel line is just a metal box.

And the cut is only the loud way a borrowed mind fails. There is a quieter way, and it is worse because you never get to declare it. A supply you rent is a supply you cannot test. Whoever owns it can see what you ask and change what comes back without telling you anything changed. The taps stay on. The dashboard stays green. The reasoning your office treats as its own has become someone else’s. No public evidence says any vendor is doing this today, and that distinction matters. But the architecture allows it, and that alone is reason enough not to build your mind on water you cannot test.

Here the obvious objection arrives, and it is a good one. A hospital can drill a well in three days. A county office cannot train a frontier model in three centuries. If owning the capability is impossible, building it into your walls sounds like a slogan with nowhere to stand.

But the choice was never own the frontier or rent the frontier. That is a false floor. A hospital does not manufacture its own municipal supply; it keeps wells and tankers and a grid connection so no single failure can leave it dry. The same shape works here. Run on more than one provider, so no single directive turns you dark. And for the decisions that fall on a human being, keep a smaller model you own and can inspect, because for those, continuity and the ability to explain yourself matter more than the last increment of horsepower. You do not need the frontier to deny someone’s benefits. You need a reason you can show them. That is a real trade, and it should be made out loud: sovereignty over the part of the building that decides about a person is worth more than its brilliance.

The storm and the shutdown are not the same in every way, and the difference is the discipline. A hurricane is indifferent; it hits everyone and passes. A directive is aimed, it can be aimed at you, and the line may never be reconnected. A hurricane you can at least see coming: the National Hurricane Center warned of catastrophic inland flooding four days out. The cut arrived with no forecast at all. You cannot watch the sky for a storm that has no season. You can only build so the capability is already in your walls before it comes.

There is a reason this scene keeps recurring across decades. Katrina drowned Memorial Medical Center’s power in 2005. Sandy knocked out NYU Langone’s backup generators in 2012. Maria left Puerto Rico without a grid for months in 2017. Helene cut Asheville’s water for ten weeks in 2024. Different storms, one shape: demand surges while the lifelines fail, and the institutions that keep operating are the ones that never outsourced the thing they could not live without.

We like to prepare for the storm. The hospitals that survive prepare for the season, because the storms do not stop and the next one is already forming over warm water. The systems deciding your life are sitting on that coast right now, on the wrong side of every clock, behind a power line someone upstream can cut whenever they choose. You can build them the other way. You can put the water in the walls. But you have to do it before the night the ambulances start arriving every two minutes, not after.
