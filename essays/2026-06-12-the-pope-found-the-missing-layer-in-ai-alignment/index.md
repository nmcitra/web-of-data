On June 12, 1982, a million people walked past the United Nations into Central Park to demand an end to the nuclear arms race — the largest political demonstration the country had yet seen. They were right about the danger and early about the remedy: you do not negotiate away a capability, you govern it. Forty-four years later the arms race has only changed substrate. It is no longer for the warhead but for the control plane of intelligence — the layer that decides who may act, on what, under which constraints, at what cost, and with whose consent. Pope Leo XIV named the task in Magnifica Humanitas: disarm AI, free it from the mentality of armed competition.

But disarming is not enough.

A weapon laid down is not a system made safe; it is a system momentarily idle. The harder fact sits underneath all of it: no system can prove its own trustworthiness from the inside. Trust has to be carried by something outside the agent — a substrate that can witness it, audit it, and refuse it.

That is the work this campaign is for.

## The date was the first signal

I read the encyclical the morning I found it, on my back patio here in Las Cruces with my usual caffe americano. The Organ Mountains still waking up behind the house. The thing I noticed, before I had read a single paragraph, was the date.

May 15, 2026.

If you do not live inside the genealogy of Catholic Social Teaching, the date looks like a publication detail — the date stamped on [the document](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html), even though the encyclical was publicly presented on May 25.

Inside the tradition, it is something else.

May 15, 1891 is the day Pope Leo XIII signed Rerum Novarum, the encyclical that founded modern Catholic thinking on labor and capital. May 15, 1931 is the day Pius XI signed Quadragesimo Anno and introduced subsidiarity into doctrine. May 15, 1961 is the day John XXIII signed Mater et Magistra.

May 15 is a Catholic Social Teaching timestamp. It is not picked. It is invoked.

Pope Leo XIV chose his regnal name to point back to Leo XIII. He chose the same calendar day to point back to Rerum Novarum. Then he wrote, on the page, that the new encyclical addresses “the new things” of our age — the same Latin phrase that names the 1891 letter. By the date alone, before a single word of the text, he had placed artificial intelligence inside the labor-capital-platform lineage. He had put it on the same shelf as the great industrial-era documents that taught the Church how to talk about concentrated economic power.

This is not a paper about a Pope’s literary cleverness. The date is a claim.

The claim is that what is happening to us with AI is structurally adjacent to what happened to factory workers in the nineteenth century — not in detail, but in kind. A new technology. A new arrangement of who owns what and who decides what. A new set of people whose dignity is being absorbed into someone else’s productivity curve. The Church has done this work before.

The calendar says it intends to do it again.

I am a security practitioner, not a theologian. I have spent the last twenty years sitting inside government systems trying to keep the wrong things from happening to the right people. I know what it looks like when an institution decides to recognize a fight it had been pretending was not there. The May 15 dateline is that.

## The word was the second signal

The second signal arrived in the verb.

At paragraph 110, Pope Leo XIV writes that artificial intelligence needs to be disarmed — freed from the mentality of “armed” competition. He did not say regulate. He did not say align. He did not say make safe. He said disarm.

He has been saying it for over a year.

From his first balcony speech as pope, on the night of his election in May 2025, he spoke of “unarmed and disarming peace.” Days later, addressing the media, he said: “Let us disarm words and we will help to disarm the world.” Now, in May 2026, the same verb shows up at the load-bearing paragraph of his first encyclical, applied to AI.

CBS, France 24, and CNN all led with the verb disarm in their headline coverage of the release. The motif is not flourish. It is the protocol the document is trying to articulate.

Disarm is a strong word. It is also a precise one. To regulate is to set rules at the edge of a thing while leaving the thing intact. To disarm is to take something away from it — specifically, the capacity to dominate. In international relations, you disarm a state by removing its weapons. In substrate terms, you disarm a system by removing its monopoly over the conditions others must live inside.

The pope’s verb does two jobs at once. Officially, it locates the encyclical in the long Catholic tradition of nuclear-disarmament teaching — the tradition that produced John XXIII’s Pacem in Terris. Operationally, it names something a frontier-lab CEO cannot quite say out loud: the problem with AI is not only what the model values, but who holds the conversion rights from raw capability to action in the world. An affordance is what the environment makes possible. An authorization is what the environment permits. Disarming AI, in the way the pope means it, is not about removing capabilities from the models. It is about ensuring no single actor holds the conversion rights from capability to legitimate action.

That is a sentence about substrate. It is, in different vocabulary, what the engineering frame I have been building calls: a control plane.

## Paragraph 71 names the new sovereign

The doctrinal mutation arrives at paragraph 71.

Subsidiarity is the principle that decisions belong at the lowest competent level. Higher levels exist to help lower ones — sub-sidium, “help from below” — not to absorb them. Pius XI introduced it formally into magisterial teaching in Quadragesimo Anno in 1931. For ninety-five years, the principle has been applied almost exclusively to one question: how much should the state do, and how much should be left to civil society — to families, local associations, parishes, unions, schools? The doctrinal debate has been about state versus non-state actors.

At paragraph 71, Pope Leo XIV moves it.

He writes, in essence, that in the digital era the “highest level” is no longer only the state. The actors with effective power over the conditions of ordinary life — over visibility, opportunity, access, attention, the data substrate decisions get made inside — are major economic and technological actors. Platforms. Frontier labs. The handful of companies that own the compute, the pipes, the model weights, and the interface through which billions of people now think.

That is one of the clearest magisterial applications of subsidiarity to digital platforms in the ninety-five years since the principle entered formal social teaching. It is the doctrinal move the encyclical may be remembered for.

What it implies is harder than what it says. Subsidiarity has always been a two-sided principle: a higher level may not absorb a lower one, and a higher level is obligated to help where the lower one cannot bear the load alone. If the highest level is now the platform, then the platform inherits the obligations that previously belonged to public authority. The descriptive sentence — the highest level is now… — implies a normative consequence the document does not fully unpack. Platforms now owe the kind of protective duty to persons that the Catholic tradition has long argued the state owes its citizens.

The gap between “platforms are the highest level” and “platforms therefore owe X to persons” is exactly the territory engineering frameworks have to claim. Doctrine can name the duty. Doctrine cannot ship the mechanism that performs it. That is not a deficiency in doctrine. That is the division of labor.

## Paragraph 107 indicts alignment-as-governance

If paragraph 71 names the new sovereign, paragraph 107 names what is wrong with how that sovereign currently behaves.

Pope Leo XIV writes, in magisterial register, that a more moral AI is not enough if that morality is determined by a few. The phrasing is careful. He is not against moral AI. He is not against the people working on it. He is naming a structural failure mode: when a small number of actors choose the values that get embedded in the substrate the rest of us have to think inside, the result is not alignment. It is concentration. It is the pattern the doctrine has been criticizing since 1891 — only the means of production now ship as model weights.

Catholic Social Teaching diagnoses the dignity violation. [Kinetic Trust Protocol](https://kinetic-trust-protocol.net/research/magnifica-humanitas) (KTP) and its Trust Force Equation (TFE) explain the control surface that produces it.

A word of care here. Alignment work is serious. Many of the best technical minds I have read are doing exactly this work, in good faith, at real personal cost. Anthropic’s interpretability team — Chris Olah’s group — has produced some of the most honest empirical work on what is actually happening inside frontier models. The RLHF literature, the constitutional AI papers, the early formal-verification efforts, the steering-vector research — these are real contributions. Nothing in the encyclical, and nothing in what I am writing, dismisses them.

What the encyclical says — and what the engineering frame I have been building has been saying from a different starting point — is that alignment is insufficient in a particular way. Not wrong. Insufficient. It treats as a technical-substrate question what is constitutionally a distributed-political question. It asks what values should the model prefer? without first asking who is authorized to set those values, on whose behalf, with what review, at what cost to whom, under whose consent? Those are not technical questions you reach later in the project. They are constitutional questions you have to answer before the technical question makes sense.

Alignment is a category error before it is a safety problem. It is treating a question about authority as if it were a question about training data.

Gilbert Ryle gave this kind of mistake a name in 1949. The visitor who tours the colleges, libraries, and quads of Oxford and then asks “but where is the university?” has not asked a harder question. He has asked a question that has no answer in the language he is using. The university is not another building on the list; it is the relation among them. Alignment is doing the same kind of work — trying to locate trustworthy AI inside the model weights, the same place all its tools already point, and then registering surprise when the answer is not there. Thomas Kuhn called this paradigm incommensurability: not that the alternative is wrong, but that it is in a different language, and the existing paradigm cannot evaluate it from inside itself. The Ptolemaic astronomer watching Mars retrograde did not need better instruments. He had the best instruments in his world. He needed a different center.

That is a hard line for the field to absorb, because it says the framing is wrong, not just the implementation. It is also exactly what the encyclical is saying, in the only register a Pope is allowed to say it in.

## Babel is a substrate problem

I want to slow down, because the next part of this argument is the part that is mine.

There is a passage Pope Leo XIV returns to throughout the encyclical: the Tower of Babel from Genesis. He reads it through Augustine — pride, self-sufficiency, the homogenization that makes one tongue into none, the communication collapse that follows when all the speakers think they are speaking the same language and discover they are not. It is a beautiful reading. It is also a partial reading, and the part that gets left out is the part I think the substrate-engineering frame most needs.

In the literal text of Genesis, the people of Babel try to build a tower “with its top in the heavens” to “make a name for themselves” and avoid being “scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.” God’s [response](https://kinetic-trust), in the text, is not punishment in the conventional sense. It is scattering. God introduces a multiplicity of languages and disperses the builders. The tower stops because there is no longer a common substrate of communication to build it inside.

Read the mechanism, not the morality. The Genesis text, before any later interpretation gets layered on top of it, reads as God restraining consolidating human power by introducing multipolarity at the substrate level.

That is not a metaphor I am stretching to fit. That is the mechanism in the text. Augustine emphasized pride because pride was the great vice he was equipped to name. The text itself emphasizes geography and language — distribution and difference. The pride is the human posture. The scattering is the substrate move.

> The substrate reading also sits inside a larger pattern the pride frame tends to flatten. The Genesis editor placed Babel in chapter eleven, immediately after the Flood — and the placement is not arbitrary. Genesis 6 opens with consolidation reaching the point where, in the text’s flat register, “every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.” The Flood is the first divine restraint on that consolidation. Babel is the second. The grammar of restraint changes — water in the first, language in the second — but the structural concern is the same: concentration of capacity, in a creature that builds, accelerates the capacity for harm faster than the creature can self-correct. The divine response is not to halt the technology. It is to interrupt the conditions under which a sufficiently concentrated power can wield it to do the worst available thing. Read in that lineage, AI is the third inflection — not a metaphor, a structural recurrence. (This Flood-Babel lineage was sharpened for me by a conversation with a trusted theologian friend — Lane Leverett, whose grounding in Genesis 6:1–13 made the pattern visible from inside the tradition. The reading is his; the engineering application is mine.)

If you take the substrate reading seriously, you discover that the theological warrant for distributed authorization is much older than any modern political philosophy. It is hiding in the version of Babel the encyclical, for entirely defensible Augustinian reasons, declined to use. Babel is what happens when one actor controls the substrate others must inhabit. Scattering is the interruption of substrate monopoly.

The frontier labs, right now, are building the tower. They mean well. Many of them are doing it carefully. But the structure of what they are building — one substrate, one set of weights, one company holding the keys to the cognitive infrastructure of a generation — is the Babel structure. Disarming, in the Genesis sense, is what scattering does. It introduces enough multiplicity at the substrate level that no single actor’s failure becomes everyone’s failure.

This is why a protocol matters more than a policy. Policy can be captured by the actor with the most lawyers. A substrate that physically distributes the conversion rights cannot be. The Genesis warrant is for the protocol layer. That is the layer the encyclical names but does not specify. That is the layer where the work has to happen.

The wilderness near my house, the Gila, includes the world’s first designated wilderness and one of the great wild landscapes of the Southwest. It is wild because no single agency holds the conversion rights to develop it. Federal land, state land, tribal land, private inholdings, water rights that go back to the [acequia governance](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/from-building-bunkers-to-planting-seeds-b8db66f24387) Hispanic settlers brought from medieval Spain. The Gila is not preserved by one actor’s good values. It is preserved by the substrate not being unified enough to be captured. That is a Genesis structure. It is also exactly the structure AI infrastructure no longer has.

## From Manning to Olah: engagement is not endorsement

The optics need addressing, because they have been misread.

When Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah walked onto the Vatican stage on May 25 to speak alongside the academic presenters of the encyclical, the easy read was capture. Pope hosts AI company. AI company gets papal cover. Frontier-lab logos drift onto Vatican letterhead. Matthew Walther made the sharpest version of this read in the New York Times the next day: “Imagine if Leo XIII had invited John D. Rockefeller to hear him speak on the dignity of labor!” I understand the reflex. I am a security person; I am paid to assume the worst about institutional optics. I do not think the capture reading survives the historical record — and the Rockefeller analogy, sharp as it is, has the wrong precedent at hand.

In 1889, Cardinal Henry Edward Manning, the Catholic Archbishop of Westminster, walked into the middle of the London Dock Strike. The dockers were striking for the “dockers’ tanner” — sixpence an hour, the minimum wage of the time. The dock owners were dug in. The strike was on the edge of collapse, with public sympathy slipping. Manning mediated. He met with the strike leaders. He met with the owners. He kept Leo XIII informed throughout. The strike was settled on terms favorable to the workers. Two years later, in 1891, Leo XIII published Rerum Novarum — the encyclical that founded modern Catholic Social Teaching, the one whose 135th anniversary Leo XIV deliberately chose for the dateline of Magnifica Humanitas.

Manning was not endorsing capital by sitting at a table with dock owners. He was entering a frontier labor conflict with moral standing — trusted by both sides because he was clearly not the property of either. The presence of the Church at the table did not anoint the owners. It changed the shape of the table.

Olah on the Vatican stage is the same move, 137 years later, applied to a different frontier. He was not there to receive a blessing. He was there as a boundary object — the term sociologists Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer coined in 1989 for an artifact that means different things to different communities while remaining recognizably the same object. To Anthropic, his presence was a sign the Vatican took the work seriously enough to argue with. To the Vatican, his presence was a sign that industry would show up and listen. To watchdog groups like the Future of Life Institute, his presence was a recognition of who is winning the race to replace human workers. Three constituencies, three readings, one artifact. None of them is wrong.

And — this is the part that gets missed — Olah used the platform to publicly disagree with the encyclical’s most contested paragraph. He gently but clearly pushed back on paragraph 99. He named, in his own remarks, that “every frontier AI lab, including Anthropic, operates inside a set of incentives and constraints that can sometimes conflict with doing the right thing.” He asked the Church to keep being an informed critic the incentives cannot bend. Then Pope Leo XIV, in his closing remarks, accepted the invitation to “walk together” — and immediately framed the encounter as dialogue “with our differences.” The presentation transcript is a public document. The video is online. It does not look like capture. It looks like Manning at the dock table — engagement with moral standing intact, public differences preserved, the doctrine still doing its work.

The Manning template gives a 137-year-old precedent for exactly the institutional move Leo XIV just made. The Church can host the builder, listen to him, publicly differ from him, and still issue a doctrine that constrains the world he is helping build. The Vatican has been doing this since the docks.

What I would add, having watched a lot of safety-and-industry conversations across twenty years of public-sector security work, is that the alternative to engagement is not purity. It is irrelevance. A doctrine that refuses to be in the room ends up addressing a world that has already left it. The encyclical is in the room. The frontier lab is in the room. The disagreement is on the record.

The next move belongs to whoever shows up next.

## Why KTP can bracket paragraph 99

There is one paragraph of the encyclical where the technical world will pick its fight.

At paragraph 99, Pope Leo XIV makes a strong claim about the nature of AI itself — that the systems we are building cannot truly think, feel, or develop morally relevant inner states. He hedges some of it. The hedge does not soften the load-bearing claim. Several careful readers — Anthropic-adjacent, secular AI-safety, and Catholic-intellectual alike — have already flagged this as the document’s most attackable surface. Chris Olah, on the Vatican stage at the May 25 presentation, gently pushed back in person. The Pillar’s live coverage flagged the same tension. The technical commentariat will spend the next year arguing about it.

This is going to be the consciousness fight. I want to register a different position from it.

The substrate-engineering frame does not need the consciousness question settled before it can govern authorization. Whether the model has inner states is a real question. It is a fascinating question. It is also a question that operates at a different layer from the question of whether an agent should be allowed to act on a resource right now. Substrate physics operates whether or not the agent has inner experience. The trust dynamics, the authorization decay, the cost of unjustified action — none of it requires resolving the consciousness question first.

This is a position only the engineering frame can credibly hold. Anthropic-adjacent readers are pressured to defend the empirical openness of the consciousness question. Conservative Catholic readers are pressured to defend the encyclical’s anthropological claim. KTP can say, with no contradiction, that whether AI has inner states is not the first governance question. The first governance question is whether an agent can act, with what authority, at what cost, under what constraints, in whose environment. Substrate physics operates regardless.

That bracketing is not evasion. It is sequencing. The constitutional question — who is authorized to convert intelligence into action — comes before the metaphysical question — what kind of thing the intelligence is. If you cannot answer the constitutional question, the metaphysical one cannot save you. If you can, the metaphysical one is a question the long-term tradition can take its time with.

The encyclical, at its strongest, is teaching the field to slow down on the metaphysics and speed up on the authority. KTP supports that move without taking either side of the consciousness debate. That is the strategically distinct position the engineering frame is in.
