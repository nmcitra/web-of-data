Six Seven
On the Speed of Propagation, and the Slowness of Repair

Your thumb stops.

You don’t decide to stop. The scroll was automatic — wrist, tendon, glass — and then something in the feed snagged a circuit older than language and your body responded before your brain caught up. A hitch in the breath. A narrowing of focus. Six seconds pass. You haven’t moved. The algorithm has already read the pause, logged the duration, weighted the signal, and begun adjusting what comes next. You resume scrolling. You don’t remember deciding to stop. You don’t remember deciding to start again.

That pause is not a glitch. It’s a measurement. And what it measures is the distance between your nervous system and your awareness of your nervous system.

A child on a playground says “six seven” to another child. The phrase has an origin somewhere in the feed, but origin is not meaning. For the child repeating it, the payload is recognition: *you know the thing; I know the thing; now we both know that we know.* The second child repeats it. Neither needs to know why. One kid says it and the next kid says it and by Thursday every eight-year-old in the school is saying it and not one of them can tell you what it carries. The phrase succeeded because propagation is what it was built for. Not built by anyone. Built by the shape of the channel it moved through.

The mechanism that carries “six seven” through a school is the same mechanism that carries a false identity through tens of thousands of mentions in hours. The payload is different. The physics is identical.

A caveat first, because the error runs both directions. Most of what spreads online is organic culture doing what culture has always done — people sharing, imitating, riffing, belonging. The overwhelming majority of viral content is not a weapon. The mistake is reading every meme as a psyop, every trending phrase as an operation. Paranoia is itself a form of the degradation this essay describes. A population that sees manipulation everywhere has already been manipulated — into seeing nothing clearly. The discipline is learning to distinguish the signal from the noise while acknowledging that the noise and the signal travel the same wires.

That discipline starts with the wires themselves.

## The Pipeline

Something crosses your visual field while you scroll. A face, a number, a flash of color. Before you know you’ve seen it, your thalamus has routed the signal — a relay station the size of a walnut, older than the cortex, faster than thought. Within a fraction of a second, fast subcortical and emotional-processing systems have begun sorting the signal before reflective awareness catches up. The amygdala doesn’t think. It reacts. It flags the signal as relevant or irrelevant, safe or threatening, before the thinking parts of your brain have received the mail. This is how you flinch before you know what startled you. This is how your thumb stops before you know what caught it.

That’s the first stage. Attention capture. Not attention in the way we use the word — deliberate, chosen, directed. The opposite. Attention seized by machinery that predates choice.

Now the signal has your body. What happens next is emotion. Not the word people use for feelings — the older thing, the neurochemical fact. Dopamine tags the signal as worth pursuing. Cortisol tags it as threatening. Oxytocin tags it as belonging. The tagging happens in milliseconds, and the tags determine everything that follows — what you remember, how you remember it, and whether you act. A signal tagged with high arousal and negative valence will be remembered more vividly, recalled more often, and shared more readily than one tagged with low arousal and positive content. Fear travels faster than joy. This is not a flaw. It kept your ancestors alive. It is, however, a targeting surface.

Third stage. Memory encoding. The signal, now tagged with emotion, gets written into the architecture. Not like a file saved to a disk — like a path worn through grass. The more the path is traveled, the deeper it gets. The first version of a story you encounter wears the deepest path. This is the [continued influence effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26147670/), and it is among the most important findings in cognitive science: your brain treats the first version as a draft it keeps consulting. Corrections arrive later, in a different format, at a different speed, and they do not overwrite the draft. They append to it. The original is still there, underneath, and when you’re tired or distracted or deciding quickly, the draft is what you reach for.

Fourth. Valuation. Your prefrontal cortex — the part that’s supposed to weigh evidence, compare options, assess credibility — gets its turn. But it gets its turn after the amygdala, after the neurochemical tags, after the memory encoding. It is working with material that has already been sorted, flagged, and filed by systems that don’t answer to reason. The weighing is real. The scales were loaded before the weighing began.

Fifth. Decision. You share or you don’t. You believe or you don’t. You act or you scroll past. The entire pipeline — capture, tag, encode, weigh, decide — can complete in under six seconds. The same six seconds your thumb was paused.

A child says “six seven.” Another child hears it. Attention captured — the phrase is novel, unexpected. Emotion tagged — positive arousal, social belonging, mild absurdity. Memory encoded — the first version, the only version, because the phrase has no correction to compete with. Valuation — the brain weighs whether to repeat it and finds no cost. Decision — the child repeats it. Total elapsed time: about the duration of a breath.

That’s the pipeline. Every signal you encounter on every screen you own passes through these five stages in this order. The pipeline is not the problem. The pipeline is what lets you learn language, read faces, fall in love, raise children, and navigate a world that would kill you in an afternoon without it. **The vulnerability and the gift are the same thing.** The social circuitry that can be exploited is the same circuitry that lets you trust a stranger’s face, believe a friend’s grief, feel your chest tighten when your child is afraid. The answer cannot be “feel less.” The answer cannot be “think more critically” — the critical thinking arrives fourth in a five-stage process that’s already three stages deep before the cortex gets a vote.

The answer has to be structural. It has to change the environment the pipeline operates in. Not the pipeline itself.

## The Softened Ground

Nobody coordinated this. That’s the hardest part to sit with.

There was no meeting. No memo. No conspiracy to soften the ground. A thousand rational actors — each one optimizing for engagement, for clicks, for time-on-site, for quarterly revenue — collectively produced a substrate where cognitive warfare becomes trivial. Not as a plan. As a byproduct. The engagement economy is a prisoner’s dilemma at civilizational scale: every platform that doesn’t optimize for attention loses to the one that does. Every newsroom that doesn’t write the inflammatory headline loses traffic to the one that does. Every app that doesn’t send the push notification loses the user to the one that does. Each actor is rational. The aggregate is catastrophic.

The ground was already softened before any adversary arrived.

Think about what a single day does to your capacity to trust the next thing you encounter. A spam email at 7 AM — offering a product you didn’t search for, from a sender who doesn’t exist. A robocall at 9 — a voice that sounds human, selling a warranty on a car you don’t own. A consent popup at 10 — a wall of text you won’t read, asking permission for things that will happen whether you consent or not. A chatbot at noon — apologizing for an inconvenience with language that mimics empathy the way a parrot mimics speech. A push notification at 2 — urgent, personal, calibrated to the precise frequency of your prior responses.

Each of these is a T-zero interaction. A trust encounter with zero trust content. The spam email asks for trust and offers nothing. The robocall simulates a human relationship that doesn’t exist. The consent popup performs a legal ritual that has nothing to do with consent. The chatbot apologizes without feeling, resolves without authority, and thanks you for a patience you didn’t offer. Each one is a withdrawal from the same account. And the account is your capacity — not your willingness, your neurological capacity — to trust the next thing.

The engagement economy didn’t break your attention. It replaced the environment your attention evolved for.

For most of human history, trust-bearing signals were constrained by cost. The person standing in front of you had walked there. **The handshake required proximity**. The promise was made with a face you could read and a body you could see. The gossip network was small enough that lies had consequences. The signals were expensive because physics made them expensive — distance, time, muscle, presence. Your brain calibrated itself to an environment where costless trust signals were rare, because costless trust signals barely existed.

Now look at the environment the pipeline operates in. Costless signals are the medium. The feed is made of them. Every interaction that simulates trust without paying the cost of trust is training your nervous system to expect nothing from the next encounter. And a nervous system trained to expect nothing is a nervous system that can’t distinguish the warning from the noise when the warning arrives.

The trust architecture before platforms worked like a honeycomb — each person’s edges of trust connected to the next, small cells holding the community, pressure distributed across a structure that bent but didn’t shatter because every cell bore a share of the load.

I watched this happen in the cyber terrain for more than twenty years. Every system that degrades follows the same arc — the costly signals get replaced by cheap ones, the environment floods with noise, and then one day a real threat arrives and the system can’t distinguish it from the ten thousand false signals that came before. The immune system isn’t broken. It’s exhausted. “Six seven” passes through the same depleted filters as the false identity, the manufactured outrage, the deepfake. The pipeline doesn’t know the difference. It was never supposed to. The environment was supposed to make the difference obvious. The environment is gone.

The environment is gone, and the next environment is being built — not by anyone, but by the same forces, on the brains that don’t have a choice yet.

A child born in February 2026 will be six years old when the world votes in 2032. Twelve when it votes in 2038. Eighteen — old enough to cast a ballot — in 2044.

The neural architecture that child brings to those elections is being built right now.

Not later. Now. Every hour of every day.

Between the ages of roughly ten and twenty-five, the human brain is running the most consequential construction project in its lifespan. The prefrontal cortex — the part that weighs evidence, inhibits impulse, holds competing ideas without collapsing into the loudest one — is the last region to mature. It is being wired during adolescence. The wiring is not automatic. It depends on the environment. A brain that spends those years navigating complex social situations, resolving conflicts face to face, reading rooms, reading bodies, making decisions with consequences and learning from the outcomes — that brain builds the dense prefrontal architecture that makes self-governance possible. A brain that spends those years in an engagement-optimized feed builds something else.

Every hour spent inside an engagement-optimized feed is an hour not spent in another training environment: face-to-face conflict, boredom, repair, embodied play, difficult conversation, shared work. The window has a biological clock, and the clock doesn’t pause while the feed runs.

I have a nephew who was born that February. When I hold him, his hand grips my finger with the reflex that every mammal is born with — grip or fall. The grip is pre-linguistic, pre-cognitive, pre-everything. It’s the body’s first trust signal, and it costs him everything he has. His entire tiny muscular output, committed to holding on.

By 2032, the feed may have had six years to train his attention system. By 2038, twelve years. The pipeline I described earlier — attention, emotion, memory, valuation, decision — is being shaped, hour by hour, by whatever environment his developing brain spends the most time inside. If that environment is a feed optimized for engagement, his attention system is being trained toward high-arousal, high-novelty signals. His memory encoding is being trained to privilege first versions. His valuation system is being trained to reward speed over accuracy, because speed is what the feed rewards. His decision-making — the part that should arrive fifth, after careful weighing — is being trained to arrive second, right after the emotional tag, because in an engagement-optimized environment, waiting is losing.

The problem is not that kids are on their phones. The problem is that their phones are the environment their brains are building themselves inside.

This is not only a metaphor about distraction. It is a developmental risk: the systems that learn attention, inhibition, social reading, and evidence-weighting are still under construction. A brain that builds itself inside an engagement-optimized feed is being shaped — at the level of which neural pathways get reinforced and which ones don’t — by the same forces the rest of this essay describes. It’s a brain where “six seven” doesn’t just pass through the pipeline. It passes through a pipeline that was tuned, during the years of maximum plasticity, by the same forces that “six seven” exploits.

And that brain votes.

In 2044, the child born this February will be eighteen. What prefrontal architecture will he bring to that ballot? What capacity to hold two competing claims and weigh them without collapsing into the one that arrived first, the one tagged with the strongest emotion, the one that tens of thousands of other people already shared? That capacity is not innate. It is built. It is built by the environment. And the environment, right now, is a feed.

## The Unnamed Harm

On April 13, 2024, a man named Joel Cauchi walked into the Westfield Bondi Junction shopping center in Sydney, Australia, carrying a knife. He stabbed and killed six people and injured twelve others before being shot by a police officer. The attack lasted approximately eleven minutes.

Soon a name was circulating. Not Cauchi’s. A different man. And the wrong name went out first not from some anonymous account but from a credentialed national broadcaster — Seven’s Weekend Sunrise named Benjamin Cohen, a Queensland man with no connection to the attack (https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-04-15/how-misinformation-spread-after-bondi-junction-stabbing/103708210), as the attacker — and from there the pipeline took it the rest of the way. His photo was circulated. His name was attached to descriptions of the attack and repeated across platform after platform — attention captured by the urgency, emotion tagged by the horror, memory encoded with a face and a name — in thousands of brains simultaneously.

This is the cleanest documented case we have of the mechanism running at scale, which is why I keep thinking about it.

Tens of thousands of mentions within hours.

From Ben Cohen’s side of the screen, the experience had a different texture. Your name, your face, connected to a mass murder. The first error wore a credential — but the thing that made it inescapable did not. The broadcast was one mistake; the pipeline turned it into tens of thousands, and not one of those repetitions called for comment, or verified anything, or did anything but pass the cargo on. The same pipeline that carries “six seven” through a school, operating at a scale and speed no individual can outrun. The correction came. The real attacker was identified. But [the continued influence effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26147670/) — the brain’s habit of treating the first version as the draft it keeps consulting — meant that the correction arrived in the wrong format. The original encoding was vivid, emotional, certain. The correction was procedural, delayed, tagged with none of the urgency that made the original stick. In millions of brains, the draft underneath never fully updated.

Walk through the pipeline. In each of those brains, the same five stages completed in the same order. Attention captured — a mass stabbing, a face, a name. Emotion tagged — fear, outrage, the need to identify a threat and make the world legible. Memory encoded — the face connected to the horror, written deep because the arousal was high and the valence was negative. Valuation — the prefrontal cortex weighed the evidence, but the evidence was a name repeated tens of thousands of times, which the brain reads not as repetition but as confirmation. Decision — share. Tag. Repost. Add commentary. The pipeline completed, over and over, in brain after brain, and each completion reinforced the encoding in the next brain that encountered it.

The incident had no classification. There is no institutional vocabulary for what happened to Ben Cohen. “Viral misidentification” is a description, not a category. “Online mob” is a metaphor, not a framework. There is no actuarial table for it, no regulatory regime, no doctrine. It exists in the gap where the unnamed passes every defense — because every defense requires a name, a category, a classification that triggers a proportional response. Without the classification, the event has no institutional address. It happened. It was corrected. It was never categorized. And what has never been categorized cannot be defended against, because defense is a function of vocabulary, and the vocabulary doesn’t have a word for this.

“Six seven” passed through the pipeline as a harmless string of sounds. Ben Cohen’s name passed through the same pipeline as an accusation of mass murder. The mechanism did not distinguish between them. It was never built to. The mechanism moves what moves. What it carries, and what that cargo does to the person on the other end — that was always the environment’s job.

## The Repair Asymmetry

The asymmetry is the whole story.

Offense is structured. Professional. Industrialized. State-sponsored influence operations run on budgets, staff, and infrastructure that would be recognizable to any military planner. AI-generated content — text, image, audio, video — now costs effectively nothing to produce and is increasingly difficult to distinguish from organic material. A single operator with a language model can generate a thousand variations of a narrative, test them against target populations, amplify the ones that propagate, and iterate. The cycle time is hours. The cost per attempt is pennies.

Defense is slow. Coalition-dependent. Expensive. Identifying a false narrative requires monitoring, analysis, verification, coordination across institutions that don’t share data and often don’t share incentives. Correcting a false narrative requires reaching the same populations that received the original — through channels that are slower, less engaging, and competing against an encoding that arrived first and burned deeper. The correction must not only reach the audience. It must overwrite a draft the brain has already filed.

One child says “six seven.” Six seconds to propagate. No cost, no effort, no intent required. Weeks to trace the origin. Months to study the mechanism. The asymmetry is not a bug. It is the system. Offense moves at the speed of the pipeline. Defense moves at the speed of institutions. The gap between those speeds is the arms race.

Autonomy cannot exceed environment. When the environment’s capacity to absorb, verify, and correct is outpaced by the autonomy of the signals moving through it, the environment degrades. Not because the signals are malicious — most aren’t. Because the volume and velocity exceed what the substrate can metabolize. The arms race is not between attackers and defenders. It’s between the speed of propagation and the speed of repair. And right now, repair is losing.

A child says “six seven” and another child repeats it and by Thursday every kid in the school is saying it and nobody knows why.

Come back to that. Hold it. The phrase is empty. That was the point — it carries no content, serves no purpose, means nothing. Its only property is that it moves. And that one property was enough. Enough to saturate a school. Enough to demonstrate that the channel is open, the pipeline is live, the mechanism works.

Now fill the phrase. Put a name in it. Put a face. Put an accusation, a fear, a lie shaped precisely to fit the pathway it needs to travel — high arousal, negative valence, first-version advantage, shared at speed by brains that are running a pipeline built to move exactly this kind of signal. The phrase was harmless. The mechanism it revealed is not.

Everything in this essay runs on that mechanism. The brain pipeline. The softened ground. The child whose prefrontal cortex is building itself inside a feed. Ben Cohen’s name appearing tens of thousands of times in hours. The asymmetry between the six seconds it takes to propagate and the weeks it takes to correct. All of it is “six seven” with different cargo.

The cargo is different. The physics is identical.

This essay is the diagnostic. The instrument you hold up to the mechanism so you can see it operating. The structural question — what to build, where to build it, what the physics of repair looks like when the physics of propagation has already won the first three rounds — is for what comes after.

Your thumb is hovering. You’ve been still for a few seconds. The feed is reading your pause, measuring your breath-length hesitation, weighting the signal. Something in the last few paragraphs caught a circuit, and your body responded before your mind caught up. That response — the hitch, the pause, the stillness — is the pipeline running. Not in someone else’s brain. In yours. Right now.

The mechanism is working. It was always working.

What else have you passed on that you didn’t choose to carry?
