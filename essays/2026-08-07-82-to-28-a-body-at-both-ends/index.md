’82 to ’28: A Body at Both Ends
On the shrinking unit of judgment: door, network, badge, act.

#### On the shrinking unit of judgment: door, network, badge, act.

The Anodyne sat a full flight of stairs above Central Avenue in Albuquerque — old Route 66 in the front windows. In 2003 I was still in school, not yet anywhere near my first IT job, and I worked the door. I checked IDs, and every so often I put somebody out. That second duty you do with your hands.

The stairs did most of the work. A mirror hung at the top of the stairwell, angled down the well, and I could watch every arrival climb before they ever saw me. Whether they used the rail. How fast they came. What the ascent looked like — steady, careful, loose, wrong. By the time someone reached the top and went for a wallet, I mostly knew.

The ID was the second check. The climb was the first.

Nobody taught me that order. The building did.

It was a true hall — long, brick down both sides, skylights with triangle frame windows and plants everywhere. Fourteen nine-foot tables, beautifully kept, and a snooker table at the back. A six-foot industrial fan pulled the cigarette smoke out. They banned smoking after I stopped working there. Epoxy tables and a bar inlaid with weird objects and pennies. Red felt and red furniture against wood tones, and at the entryway the most ragged couches in town, which looked cool to somebody once. As bars went, it was mellow. And the juke box, well that thing was stuffed with thousands of CDs that the managers curated by hand. We’d pull quarters from the register to keep the music going when no one else did.

The ID got you in. It never once kept anyone in.

Staying in was a different economy: conduct, watched continuously, by a small set of people who knew the room — the bartender, the manager on duty, me, a few regulars near the doorway. And when that economy failed, the enforcement was flesh. Mine. Walking a grown man down a flight of stairs is a decision your body gets to co-sign.

One night I asked a regular to leave. The call wasn’t even mine — the bartender had cut him off; I carried it out. Here is what that removal did not do: end anything. We became friends afterward, the kind who stand around arguing about which Tool songs are the best ones. The night got judged. He didn’t. He came back, and the door was the same door.

Mercy did most of what the rules couldn’t, and mercy lives in other humans. You can’t find it in any record of the night. You detect it the way astronomers detect dark matter — by the way things curve around it. The removal that didn’t cost a friendship. The refusal that ended with the manager walking someone out gently. Weight you can only measure by its effects. Emergence.

There were appeals, too. Wrong direction for an ID, too far gone, refused at the top of the stairs — people argued, and I’d call the manager on duty, who almost always affirmed the call and saw them out himself. Which sounds like a rubber stamp until you notice what it actually was: a second human, face to face, inside a minute. The appeal rarely changed the verdict. It changed what the verdict was made of — witnessed, not just enforced.

On slow days I studied for my CCNA at the door. Spanning-Tree Protocol and routing tables at the top of the stairs. The next paradigm sat in my lap while I worked the previous one. At the time it was just a study guide.

In 2006, an account manager named Richard Vasquez, a dear friend still, sent me out on my first real firewall job. The Cisco ASAs had just come out — the perimeter’s newest gate — and somebody’s network needed one up in Santa Fe.

On the way, I called my mentor, Charlie Winkless. He asked me one question: what’s your scope of work?

I asked him back: *what’s a scope of work?*

From then on Charlie knew exactly how much help the new guy was going to need, and he gave it. Richard had staked his own name sending an untested tech to a client. Charlie carried me while I built a track record that could carry itself. I had no words for any of this at the time. I was standing inside the oldest arrangement there is: someone vouching, someone mentoring, a newcomer running on borrowed standing until he’d earned his own. I thought I was learning firewalls.

I’ve come to love the question I asked that day. *Which acts, exactly, am I authorized to perform here?* I asked it out of pure ignorance in 2006. It took our industry about twenty years to start asking it on purpose.

By 2023 I knew what a scope of work was. That year I published an essay explaining Zero Trust with an airport analogy — verify the traveler, verify the device, trust nothing by default. I described it with enthusiasm, and the enthusiasm was real. John Kindervag had named the thing back in 2010, and it was the right correction at the right time: the perimeter had dissolved, and pretending otherwise was getting people breached.

I learned a long time ago what the goal underneath it was: least privilege. Give every identity the smallest workable set of permissions, and verify relentlessly. It was an architecture, and architectures become layers to build on. The door I worked didn’t disappear when firewalls came; it became one input among many. Firewalls didn’t disappear under Zero Trust; they became signals feeding a smarter gate. Nothing in this story gets replaced. Each layer gets built upon.

But while I was explaining airports with real conviction, another part of my work sat quietly disagreeing.

The fraud years started with the pandemic. When the relief money moved, the fraud moved with it, and I went looking for the people doing it — in their own forums, on the darknet, where the methods were written down.

That’s the part that rearranged me: *written down.* Tutorials. Playbooks. Step by step, some with better documentation than the organizations I was helping defend. The sophistication everyone kept invoking in post-mortems was sitting there, published, available to anybody willing to do the reading.

I hear the phrase “sophisticated fraud” differently now. It’s a label that makes a loss sound inevitable. Most of the time it was covering for something simpler — a defense that lacked the creativity to see a pattern sitting in its own data. Fraud is a choice; calling it sophisticated was usually ours.

I say that having used the phrase myself. The reading was available. I did the reading.

Fraud is also where I met the thing the airport couldn’t hold. An account takeover *is* a valid session — real credential, right password, healthy device, wrong human. The checkpoint has no lane for that. And the fraud world had already spent decades not caring, because it never trusted the credential in the first place. Every card swipe you have ever made was judged on its own merits, at the moment it happened, in milliseconds. The card gets you nothing. The transaction is what’s judged.

Fraud’s judgments had two properties the rest of security rarely had. They were denominated — every miss had a dollar amount and a named bearer, someone who ate the loss. And they were fresh. A judgment’s timestamp relative to its outcome is what separates evidence from theater, and fraud was the one discipline that couldn’t afford theater. I eventually gave the freshness a name and a metric: Time to Good Decision, the value of a decision decaying with the time it takes to make it well.

The teams I worked alongside were living it long before I named it.

These days, part of my work is a project that takes place in 2028 — the Games. Stadiums full of people, and the question of how to keep them safe.

I was driving when I started thinking it through, and I did what I suppose I have always done: reached backward to reason forward. I had just been to a Metallica show in September 2025. I pulled the memory up and put myself back inside it — and instead of the stage, I saw my own trail. The path I had traced from the parking lot to the pit.

Then the trail became motion. The motion became trajectory. The trajectory had mass.

And what’s not mass is Environment.

It arrived exactly that fast — as seeing, not thinking. The crowd resolved into moving masses, each dragging its history behind it, and everything that was not moving mass was the environment the masses moved through. Two things exhaust the scene: the thing acting, and the thing holding.

The moment it came to me — driving through Las Cruces less than a year ago, I thought about the pot and its empty space.

The realization that what holds value is the structure around the absence. The stadium was the same flip in different clothes.

What’s not mass is what holds the mass.

And notice what did not happen in that vision. Not one gate failed. A stadium is the checkpoint’s home turf — tickets, wands, pat-downs — and I have no quarrel with any of them. I just stopped seeing gates. The object of security changed in front of me: from the perimeter to the trails, from the credential to the motion, from the gate to whatever it is that holds.

I realized afterward that I’d spent my career walking down the same staircase.

Start at the top.

Physical security: the thing being judged is a body at a door, the grant lasts as long as the job, and the judge is a person reading a face. That was me in 2003, with a mirror.

One step down, the perimeter: the thing judged is an address — are you inside the network? — the grant lasts the connection, and the judge is the topology itself. That was me in 2006, bolting on the new gates.

Another step, identity: the thing judged is a principal — are you who you claim, on a healthy device? — the grant lasts the session, and the judge is an identity provider consulted at session start. That was me in 2023, enthusiastically writing about airport security.

One more step, and you reach the action: the thing judged is one act. The grant lasts one act. **The judge is the environment, now.** That’s every card swipe you’ve ever made. The fraud world has been standing at the bottom of the staircase the whole time, wondering what took the rest of us so long.

People in my field count paradigms and argue about the count. The count is bookkeeping — the direction is the finding. Three quantities shrink in lockstep as you descend:

- the size of the thing judged,
- the lifetime of the grant, and
- the staleness of the judgment when it’s finally spent.

Room, network, principal, act.\
Career, connection, session, single act.

A judgment made once about a hire; a judgment made this millisecond about this card swipe.

And every step above the bottom was a proxy. “Inside the building” stood in for safe. “Inside the network” stood in for safe. “Known principal, clean device” stood in for safe. Each layer judged something easier to check than the thing it actually cared about. The bottom of the staircase stops proxying and asks the question directly: **can this environment bear this act, now?** Judgment at the grain of the single act — *act-grain*, if you want a word for it, and your credit card has been living there for decades.

At the bottom, the question also splits in two.

1.  *Can* the environment bear this act — capacity, physics, the rail I work on.
2.  *Should* this act happen at all — appropriateness, proportion, context.

That second rail is the judgment the guard at the door used to provide for free, with his eyes — judgement that was eventually replaced in digital speak. Fellow collaborator Robin Martherus, who spent thirty years building the identity layer, works on that rail now — he calls it [Tamed Autonomy ](https://www.tamedautonomy.ai/)— and the honest division is that neither rail is sufficient without the other.

Once you see the staircase, you see it everywhere, because security didn’t invent it.

- Food stamps: the grocer who knew the father spending his family’s vouchers, then the EBT card, scored per swipe.
- Credit: the banker who knew your family, then the collateral, then the credit file — a score computed once and replayed for years, a session token for money — and then the per-swipe judgment.
- Employment is walking it now: the guild, the lifetime post, the at-will salary, the per-ride rating.
- Insurance: the mutual-aid society, the annual policy, the per-mile telematics.
- Political authority: the throne held for life, the constitutional term, the recall, the single-issue referendum.
- Criminal supervision: the outlaw’s lifetime mark, the sentence, the parole check-in, the ankle monitor.

Same three quantities shrinking. Same migration of the judge — away from a person who knows you, toward an environment that watches you.

Read that list again, and notice where your stomach turns.

Now hold two facts side by side.

Agentic systems — software that acts on your behalf instead of waiting for your click, at machine speed, thousands of consequential acts in a session — mostly run, today, on standing API keys and tokens that live for weeks or months. Judged once, trusted for a season. Human-length grants.

Humans — who form trust at human speed, and who need institutions that remember them across one bad day — are increasingly judged per ride, per transaction, per mile, continuously. Machine-length grants.

Each is holding the grant that belongs to the other.

The staircase has no morality of its own. The sign comes from the match between the grant and the clock of the thing being judged. A machine acting at machine tempo needs *act-grain* judgment, because a season of standing trust compounds faster than any oversight can notice. A person needs the long grant, because the long grant is an institution’s way of saying: one night will not erase you. My pool hall knew that. It judged nights, and it kept people.

So the conclusion I actually hold is the opposite of what this staircase might seem to sell. Act-grain judgment belongs on the machines precisely so that people can keep their long grants. We are running it backwards.

The last seam announced itself before it had a name. In 2009, an attacker worked comfortably inside a perimeter that held; in 2013, a fully credentialed insider walked out the front door. *The perimeter held; we lost anyway.* The vocabulary had been straining for years by then — “de-perimeterization” was coined in 2004, and the standard that finally answered it landed in 2020. The words strain about a decade before the architecture moves.

I can hear the words straining now. Delegation drift. Runtime authorization. Identity-era grammar bending around action-era problems.

When this seam’s incident comes, every agentic AI in the swarm will have a valid identity, every credential scoped, every control green — and the harm will arrive by composition, permitted acts stacking into an outcome nobody permitted. *Every control passed; we lost anyway.* I wrote that post-mortem in advance this summer, as a story about a swarm younger than the questions our controls know how to ask. I’d rather be wrong. The direction says I won’t be.

Follow any chain of trust far enough down and you do not find a certificate. You find a person.

Someone vouches for the newcomer. Someone vouched for the voucher. At the bottom, always, is a named human whose accountability lives outside the system — legal, professional, reputational, the kind that survives the database being wrong.

Fraud knew this all along. It’s the one discipline where every loss had a name attached and a dollar amount, where somebody ate it. A real judgment needs a denomination, every denomination needs a reserve, and the reserve behind every judgment currency we have ever run has been a body.

Which is where the staircase ends. The physical era put a body at the door to answer one question: *do you belong here?* Forty years of architecture abstracted that body away — the door became a firewall, became a badge, became a token.

And at the bottom of the descent, the body comes back. Different post. The door answered belonging. The root answers consequence.

I have held both jobs. The kid at the top of the Anodyne’s stairs, reading the climb in a mirror, and the named party at the root of a chain, answerable for what runs above my name. Twenty-five years apart, same posture: a person, standing where the consequence lands, answering for the room.

So — which layer would you rather be wrongly judged by? The person who can see your face, hear your appeal, and let the night go? Or the environment that scores every act, forgets nothing, and offers no one to argue with? I’m not going to answer that. I can’t.

Here’s the other question I can’t answer. Every lock we have ever built could be picked by someone with enough means: a stolen credential, a shell corporation — a clean principal built to take the judgment its owner should have. Fraud at scale was rarely about breaking the lock. It was about satisfying it. The locks kept out the small and waved through the strong.

Act-grain judgment changes that bargain. The credential no longer ends the inquiry; the act still has to answer for itself against the environment it enters. That doesn’t make judgment unpickable — conduct can be imitated, divided, coerced, and misread. But it removes the old guarantee: a clean badge carrying a dirty act through untouched.

So what happens when the powerful can no longer buy distance between the credential and the consequence?

I don’t know that either. But I know who decides what it becomes. We build these systems. We design them. We architect them — for us, and for our communities.

In 2028, if the trajectory holds, I’ll be somewhere in a stadium at the Games. That’s the only honest way to talk about a future self — as a trajectory, as a probability. The man in that concourse is an anticipated version of me, the way the crowd’s trails in my vision were anticipated versions of the crowd.

Memory at one end. Probability at the other. And at both ends of everything I have ever worked on — the door, the chain, the worldline itself — a body.

Chris Perkins
