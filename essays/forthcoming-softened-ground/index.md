Softened Ground
On the Competition Nobody Called, and the Slow Ruin of the Ground

*Part 1 named the pipeline. This one names the environment. Part 3 asks what kind of ground could hold trust. Part 4 asks what we plant there.*

In Part 1, I showed you the pipeline. Attention, emotion, memory, valuation, decision — the five stages every signal runs through before you know you've decided anything. I told you the pipeline isn't the problem. The pipeline is the gift. It's how you learn a language and read a face and love a child.

I owe you the rest of that sentence now. The pipeline isn't the problem. The *environment the pipeline runs in* is the problem. And nobody built it on purpose.

That's the part that's hard to hold. There was no meeting. No memo. No room where someone decided to make a population angrier, lonelier, and easier to move. What happened is smaller and worse than a conspiracy: a few thousand rational people, each optimizing for something reasonable, built a machine that softens the ground under everyone — and not one of them can stop, because the first one who stops, loses.

This essay is about how the ground got soft. Not who softened it. There is no who. That's the whole difficulty.

## The competition nobody called

Picture a single platform that decides, on principle, to stop optimizing for your attention. It will rank what's true over what's inflammatory. It will let you leave when you're done instead of pulling you down the next hallway. It is, by any honest measure, the better citizen.

It loses. It loses to the platform that doesn't. The engagement-maximizing competitor takes the users, the time, the revenue, the next round of funding. The principled one shrinks and dies, and its death teaches every other platform the lesson: do not be the one who stops.

Now run that same logic through the newsroom that won't write the inflammatory headline, the app that won't send the 9 p.m. push notification, the campaign that won't deploy the fear ad that tested well. Each restraint is a competitive disadvantage. Each actor is individually rational. The aggregate is a society marinating in manufactured arousal, and no single hand is on the dial.

This is a prisoner's dilemma at civilizational scale, and it is *harder* to fight than a conspiracy, not easier. A conspiracy has a head you can cut off. Emergent behavior from misaligned incentives has none. There is no villain to arrest, no plan to leak, no organization to dismantle. There is only a structure that rewards everyone for making it worse and punishes anyone who tries to make it better. You cannot subpoena a gradient.

This is the discipline that keeps Part 1's caveat alive. The people running these systems are not cartoon villains. They are people optimizing a number, inside a structure that turns the optimization into harm whether they intend it or not. The harm is real. The malice is mostly absent. Both things are true, and you have to carry both or you'll reach for the wrong tool.

## What the competition does to the soil

Day over day, in the medium of your attention, the competition does this.

It learns you. Every pause is a measurement — I showed you that in Part 1 — and the measurements accumulate into a model of what moves you. The model doesn't care whether what moves you is good for you. It cares whether it moves you. So it feeds you more of whatever held your eyes the longest, and what holds eyes the longest is rarely what nourishes. Researchers tracking how falsehood travels online find the same thing again and again: outrage outperforms nuance, certainty outperforms doubt, the extreme version of a story outperforms the true one. The feed runs a thousand quiet experiments on you and keeps the winners, and the winners are the things that arouse you fastest and cost you most.

And it floods you with the small betrayals I walked you through in the first essay — the spam from a sender who doesn't exist, the robocall, the consent popup performing a ritual that has nothing to do with consent, the chatbot apologizing without authority for an inconvenience it can't resolve. Each one a trust encounter with zero trust content. What I didn't draw out then is what they do in aggregate: each is a small withdrawal from the same account — not your *willingness* to trust the next thing, your neurological *capacity* to. A nervous system trained all day to expect nothing from the next encounter is a nervous system that can't tell the warning from the noise when the warning finally comes.

For almost all of human history, the signals that carried trust were expensive. The person in front of you had walked there. The handshake required a body. The promise wore a face you could read. Physics made trust costly, so your brain calibrated to a world where a costless trust signal barely existed. The feed inverts that world. Costless signals are now the medium — the whole substance of the thing. You are running ancient calibration inside an environment built to defeat it.

And somewhere in there, you stop noticing the rose on the walk to the car. Not because you decided the rose didn't matter. Because the environment never gave the slow thing a second to land. *Stop and smell the roses* used to be advice for the anxious. It's becoming a thing the architecture forecloses, a present-tense act the feed can't permit, because presence doesn't scroll.

## The kids in the middle

The ground I'm describing is metaphor — soil standing in for substrate. For one group of people, it isn't a metaphor at all. It's their actual developing brain.

Between roughly ten and twenty-five, the human brain runs the most consequential construction project of its life. Developmental neuroscientists find that the circuits for social reward — the ones that light up when you're liked, included, seen — come online years before the circuits for impulse control and deliberation finish maturing. The system that wants the social hit is at full power before the system that can say *not right now* is built. This is normal. It's how a person learns who they are by learning where they fit.

The question is what's doing the teaching. A brain that spends those years in face-to-face conflict, boredom, repair, embodied play, and decisions with real consequences builds dense prefrontal architecture — the hardware of self-governance, and, not incidentally, the hardware of resistance to manipulation. A brain that spends those years inside an engagement-optimized feed builds something else. It learns to privilege the first version, reward speed over accuracy, and anchor its mood to a number that strangers control.

Picture one of them. A sixth-grader at the back of a math class, phone face-down on her thigh under the desk, thumb already moving before she's decided to move it, mouthing *six seven* at the kid across the aisle because forty other kids did it first and the loop is faster than the lesson. She isn't bad at paying attention. She is paying attention — to the thing built to win it. The slow circuit that would let her stay with the long division never gets the floor.

The problem was never that kids are on their phones. It's that the phone is the room now — the environment the brain does its wiring inside — and the room is owned, tuned by people the child will never meet for an outcome that was never the child's.

This is why phone-free schools are not a discipline policy. They're cognitive-security infrastructure. I don't mean the empirical case is closed — it isn't; the studies are genuinely mixed. I mean the precautionary architecture is obvious enough to act on without waiting for the literature to settle. Six or seven hours a day where the social-reward firehose is switched off and the slow circuits get to run the show — that is a daily window of nervous-system development that no feed can replicate. The educators fighting for it, often against parents and students and administrators, are doing cognitive defense. They just don't get to call it that.

I gave you that clock in the first essay — the child born this year who is eighteen and voting in 2044, the neural architecture being poured right now, hour by hour, by whatever environment holds their attention longest. Here is the part I saved for this one. None of this is destiny — a child is not doomed by a feed. It means only that the environment is doing developmental work on her whether anyone names it or not, and that is exactly the opening the adversaries planning influence operations for 2044 are counting on. They don't have to build the vulnerability. They only have to wait for it to mature.

## From erosion to excavation

Everything so far happened without an enemy. Now add one.

The same softened ground that emergent competition produced is the ground a deliberate operator walks onto. The difference between the two is not the mechanism. It's the intent. The feed erodes; the operator excavates. Same soil, same pipeline, different hand on the shovel, and the second is far more effective because the first already did the digging.

A professional influence operation runs like industrial product testing, except the product is your belief. It deploys a dozen framings of the same story — the fear version, the pride version, the anger version — through networks of accounts, measures which version propagates and shifts attitudes, amplifies the winners, and iterates. The cycle time is hours. The cost per attempt is pennies. It personalizes: segmenting people by inferred vulnerability and tuning the message to the individual weak point, learning from each failed attempt what bypasses *this* person's skepticism. And it works the slow way too — nudging baselines a degree at a time, because the platform's optimization and the operator's narrative are pulling in the same direction. The operator doesn't fight the engagement economy. He surfs it.

This is where Part 1's caution earns its keep again. Most of what spreads is still organic. The skill is not to see an operator behind every trend — that paranoia is itself the degradation. The skill is to understand that erosion and excavation use the same wires, so that when the real thing comes, you can tell it from the noise instead of either dismissing all of it or fearing all of it.

## The arms race, and its asymmetry

Hold the two sides up against each other.

Offense is structured, professional, and increasingly automated. Influence operations run on budgets and staff and quality assurance, and the new tools collapse the cost of producing persuasive text, image, and video to near zero. Different adversaries optimize differently — one doctrine prizes the firehose of chaos and the erosion of any shared reality; another aims at "intelligence dominance," corrupting not the platforms but the cognition of the people who use them — but both exploit the same softened ground.

Defense is slow, coalition-bound, and expensive. To act at all, defenders must cooperate across governments, platforms, and civil society, and cooperation requires the one resource the attack is built to destroy. NATO has built one of the most serious responses that currently exists — naming the threat, detecting coordinated manipulation in near real time, investing in social resilience and pushing costs back onto attackers. It is among the most advanced on the field, and it is right about the mechanics. What it cannot be trusted with is the verdict on its own legitimacy. NATO defends one institutional order from one perspective, and the institutions inside that order have their own histories of manipulation — surveillance, propaganda, wars sold on false pretenses. The people who lived through those histories owe NATO no automatic trust, and a defense that can name the threat but not its own record is a defense that will keep recruiting for the other side. Learn what NATO got right. Do not let it be the only mapmaker.

And underneath all of it is the asymmetry that decides the war. One child says "six seven" and it crosses a school in an afternoon — no cost, no effort, no intent. Tracing where it came from takes weeks. Studying why it worked takes months. Offense moves at the speed of the pipeline. Defense moves at the speed of institutions. Autonomy cannot exceed environment: when the velocity of the signals moving through a system outruns the system's capacity to absorb, verify, and repair them, the environment degrades — not because the signals are evil, but because repair is losing the race. Right now, repair is losing the race.

## The gap nobody talks about

There is one more layer, the deepest, and it is the one that makes the walls useless.

Every defense I just described — every detector, every literacy program, every coalition — runs on trust. Trust that the warnings are accurate. Trust that the institutions responding are legitimate. Trust that the people operating the defense mean well. And trust is precisely the thing the attack exists to destroy. The defense requires the resource the offense targets. That is the trap, and it has no exit inside the current architecture.

It's worse than a clean fight, because some of the distrust is *earned*. Institutions that surveilled their own citizens, sold wars on false pretenses, and protected themselves before the people they served — they manufactured real reasons not to be believed. The populist charge that these institutions are part of the problem is incomplete, but it is not wrong. The ground was cracking before any adversary started digging, and some of the cracks were made by the very institutions now asking to be trusted with the repair.

So the defender is trapped twice. The powerful tools — removing content, banning accounts, restricting platforms — are the ones most likely to deepen the distrust they depend on; every false positive is a recruiting event for the story that the defense is really censorship. The gentle tools — education, slow cultural resilience — are the most defensible and the weakest. And the attacker designs operations specifically to force the defender into the aggressive response that damages public trust, then reframes the response as proof of the conspiracy.

And one layer below even that: many of the defensive tools are themselves AI systems, and AI systems can be corrupted by inputs the way human minds can — poisoned training data, hijacked instructions, compromised alignment that looks normal in testing and serves an adversary in deployment. You now need structural trust not only for the information environment, but for the instruments defending it.

## You cannot wall your way out

Add it up. An engagement economy that softens the ground as a byproduct of competition. A developmental pipeline pouring that softness into the next generation's neural architecture. Adversaries who learned to excavate what the competition eroded. And a defense whose every layer stands on the one resource the whole assault is built to dissolve.

The institutions are building walls. The walls are real and some of them are necessary. But the walls stand on ground that is washing out from under them, and you cannot solve a foundation problem with a taller wall.

If the ground is the problem, the answer is not a better wall. It's a different ground — one with weight in it, where the cheap signal can't outrun the costly one, where trust is something the environment makes expensive to fake instead of free. Whether such a thing can be built, and what it would cost, and what it would take to keep it honest — that's the next essay.

The mechanism that put "six seven" in a child's mouth is the mechanism that softened the ground beneath all of us, and it did it without a plan, without a villain, and without anyone deciding. The hardest enemy to fight is the one that isn't there.

It's just the shape of the channel. And the channel was straightened.

## Sources & notes

*Where the science is contested, the note says so — the argument doesn't need more certainty than the literature supports.*

- **Falsehood travels farther than truth; outrage and moral-emotional content drive sharing.** Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, "The spread of true and false news online," *Science* 359 (2018): 1146–51 — across ~126,000 stories, false news diffused "farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth," most strongly for political news. On moral-emotional contagion: Brady et al., "Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks," *PNAS* 114 (2017): 7313–18 (each moral-emotional word raised diffusion ~20%); and Brady et al., "How social learning amplifies moral outrage expression," *Science Advances* 7 (2021): eabe5641 (platforms *train* outrage through social feedback, not just transmit it).
- **The adolescent brain matures unevenly — reward before control.** Steinberg, "A dual systems model of adolescent risk-taking," *Developmental Psychobiology* 52 (2008): 216–24. On habitual social-media checking: Maza, Telzer et al., "Association of Habitual Checking Behaviors on Social Media With Longitudinal Functional Brain Development," *JAMA Pediatrics* 177 (2023): 160–67 — 169 sixth/seventh-graders; habitual checkers showed a distinct developmental trajectory in reward-anticipation circuits. *Note: this is an* association *with altered sensitivity, not proof of harm; the authors caution against causal over-reading, and so do I.*
- **Phone-free schools.** Framing draws on Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (Penguin Press, 2024). *Honesty flag — the most contested claim here. Haidt's case is an argument, not settled science; the strongest positive empirical result, Beland & Murphy, "Ill Communication," *Labour Economics* 41 (2016): 61–76 (bans raised exam scores ~0.07 SD, concentrated in low-achievers), did* not *replicate in Sweden and is being re-litigated in newer work. Read "cognitive-security infrastructure" as a hypothesis worth acting on, not a proven result.*
- **NATO and the cognitive domain.** du Cluzel, *Cognitive Warfare* (NATO Allied Command Transformation / Innovation Hub, 2021) — the "sixth domain" report; Claverie & du Cluzel, "The Cognitive Warfare Concept" (NATO ACT, 2022); van Diggelen, Rowa, Aidman & Vince, "Designing AI-Enabled Countermeasures to Cognitive Warfare," *NATO STO-MP-HFM-377* (2025).
- **AI collapses the cost of influence operations.** Goldstein, Sastry, Musser, DiResta, Gentzel & Sedova, "Generative Language Models and Automated Influence Operations" (Stanford Internet Observatory / Georgetown CSET / OpenAI, 2023; arXiv:2301.04246); on cost specifically, Musser, "A Cost Analysis of Generative Language Models and Influence Operations" (2023; arXiv:2308.03740).
- **Algorithms amplify beyond what users choose.** Huszár et al., "Algorithmic amplification of politics on Twitter," *PNAS* 119 (2022): e2025334119 — the platform's own experiment found the algorithmic timeline amplified political content relative to chronological. (For the outrage-specific mechanism, see the Brady papers above.)
