El Mundo
On the third language, and what harmony sounds like before anyone translates it

I’m writing this from 37,000 feet above the Atlantic Ocean.

Between two continents. Between two languages. Between the poem in my backpack and the home where I’ll read it out loud, once I brush up on enough Spanish to try.

Eleven hours gate to gate, Madrid to Dallas, then a short hop to El Paso and the last mile home to Las Cruces. Most of the cabin is asleep. All the window shades are shut. The ambient light overhead is a dim blue. It’s midday but you wouldn’t know it in here.

I’m wide awake on two americanos from the gate, listening to *What Now? with Trevor Noah*. His cohost Eugene and their guest, Dr. Rachel Yehuda, a neuroscientist who studies how trau*ma* lives in the body, are talking about psychedelics and how they bypass language to reach something underneath it. The episode was called: The Biology of What We Carry.

In my backpack, inside a paper sleeve, is a typewritten poem I can’t read. At least not completely. It’s in Spanish. It was written for me yesterday on a sidewalk in Madrid by a woman I’d never met. It’s titled *El Mundo*. The World.

When was the last time you understood something you didn’t have words for?

I need to tell you how it happened.

Friday afternoon, late in the day. The sunniest day since I’d arrived four days ago. The last few had been cold, cloudy, rain and wind pushing through the streets. But this afternoon the sky opened. Light clouds, a crisp cold, and the sun keeping everyone warm enough to slow down.

I’d just walked through Puerta del Sol. Thousands of people in every direction, the plaza wide and bright, and I was headed somewhere specific. A gift shop I’d found on the Internet, chosen because it seemed to have the most selection. Christmas ornaments for the family. A shirt for my new nephew. A bookmark and mementos for my daughters. There were dozens of shops closer but I walked past all of them.

I turned onto Calle del Arenal. Narrower. The buildings rose on both sides, stone facades catching the late afternoon light on their upper floors while the street below stayed cool in shadow. The walls on this street carry centuries in them. Moorish brickwork underneath Bourbon stone, Islamic craft layered beneath French influence, none of it erased, all of it encoded in the same facades. Three empires speaking at once and nobody translating.

There I was, first time in Spain. A man from New Mexico walking alone through Madrid at the end of a work trip, looking for gifts to bring home.

I’ve been writing and thinking about forgiveness lately.

Not in the abstract. Something specific. A series of essays about what happens when environments hold and what happens when they let go. About systems that punish forever and people who forgive anyway. A friend of the family, a lawyer named Denali who works in Las Cruces, and I are writing part of it together. The work has been surfacing things I didn’t expect. I’ve been carrying it for weeks. Not as an idea. As weight. Turning it over on long walks and longer flights. Forgiveness of myself. Of my mother, my father. It feels like the growing sense that collectively, not just personally, we will all need to learn this. Not as philosophy. As practice. As something the body does when the mind finally lets go.

I didn’t go looking for it in Madrid. It was already with me when I arrived.

Halfway down Calle del Arenal, just past the side road of Pasadizo de San Ginés, a tiny book vendor had set up along the sidewalk. Folding tables with stacks of used books, paperbacks fanning out in uneven rows. And next to the vendor, in front of a heavy wooden door, a woman sat behind a small table draped in fuchsia cloth with a blue rectangular fabric the size of her table.

She had a typewriter and a stack of envelopes and paper.

Not a laptop. Not a tablet. A typewriter. Mechanical, cream-colored, the kind where you can hear each letter land. She had brown hair, bangs, pulled up loosely. A patterned scarf in warm tones wrapped at her neck. Tan coat over a grey sweater and hoop earrings that caught the light when she moved.

Two hand-made signs leaned against the base of her table. The left one, painted in color: *Fábrica de Poemas.* A poem factory. The right one, handwritten in marker: Aquí y ahora traduzco tu corazón en poesía personalizada. Tu mensaje del alma. Regala(te) esta experiencia única. Elige el tema o no lo elijas, pero decide vivir esta aventura.

Growing up in New Mexico gave me enough Spanish to order food, ask for directions, handle the basics. Not as much as I’d like, but I’m always learning. I could read enough of her sign. Here and now I translate your heart into poetry. Your message from the soul. Choose the theme or don’t choose it, but decide to live this adventure.

She was talking to someone as I first noticed her. I’d have to wait. So I circled through the book vendor, looking at the variety of books this seller had displayed, orbiting.

As I approached, I hear a loud “hola!”

A man was sitting on the ground near the entrance. Round head, shaved close. Salt and pepper beard, fuzzy, uneven. An off-white sweater that used to be cleaner. He was holding a clear plastic cup with a single coin in it. He waved the cup at everyone who passed and said “Hola.” Most people kept walking.

I said “Hola” back. Something passed between us that wasn’t in the word. I don’t know how else to say it. He looked at me. I looked at him. We understood each other before either of us said anything else.

I made my circle through the books. The poet freed up. I stepped to her. At the exact same moment, the man rose from the ground and came to my left side.

“Hola, will you please write me a poem?”

She asked me: “What is the theme?”

He was beside me now, persistent, moving the cup, pointing at my pocket. The coin made a sound against the plastic each time he gestured. I didn’t have coins. I didn’t have cash. He repeated the same phrase over and over in Spanish. *¿Me puedes comprar algo para comer?* Can you buy me something to eat? Not money. Food. A body asking for what a body needs.

I looked at the poet. I looked at him.

“What does this man make you feel?”

She paused.

“Forgiveness and love,” I said.

The essays I’ve been writing ask a question I keep coming back to: what determines whether an environment holds or lets go? Some systems sense that conditions have changed and release. Most don’t. Most just keep holding. The filing cabinet gets heavier. The weight becomes the identity. The conviction becomes the person. The debt becomes the name.

I’d been carrying that question across an ocean and into a plaza in a country where I don’t speak the language. And when the beggar arrived at my left side at the exact moment I arrived at the poet, I felt the answer before I could articulate it.

Love came from him. The feeling I had standing there, unable to help, was not pity. It was compassion with no lever. I felt his need. I had nothing to offer but presence. The guilt of that, the ache of being full and unable to give, arrived as she began to type and I recognized it as love.

¿Me puedes comprar algo para comer?

The coin jumped around in the cup again. And again. And again.

The destination put me on the path. The path put me in front of the typewriter. The beggar gave me the second theme. I arrived carrying forgiveness. He handed me love on the way in.

He left. I don’t know when exactly. Somewhere between the themes and the typing, the plaza with its hundreds of people collapsed to two. The poet and me. The coin sound replaced by keystrokes. Fast. Confident. She knew her way around that machine, hitting the newline mechanism without looking, her fingers moving the way a musician’s do, not searching for the keys but knowing where they lived.

My senses sharpened. The street noise fell away. If you listened closely, you could hear the long sound of her finger pressing the key, the letter’s arm swinging to the page and recalling its position. Then another. Then another. Infrequently the entire typewriter would reset to a new line, the carriage snapping back to begin again. I got on one knee on the cobblestone ground to take a photo which I asked her permission before she began creating.

Her eyes locked to the page. Her hands kept moving. The fuchsia cloth, the blue fabric, the cream-colored typewriter, the late sun catching her earrings. I experienced my poem come into existence letter by letter. Sound by sound. Beat by beat.

She dated it: 200226.

She titled it: El Mundo.

Her name was Sara. I gave her forgiveness and love. She gave me back the world.

When she finished, Sara pulled the page from the typewriter and read it to me.

In Spanish. A full poem, read at the speed of feeling. Far beyond my basics.

We locked eyes. She read with her whole body. At certain lines, her hand moved to her chest, touching her heart, holding it there for a beat before continuing. She was showing me where the poem lived in her. Definitely where it came from. I didn’t need the translation. The hand on the heart was the translation.

We both had a moment. Two strangers, one typewriter, and something that moved between us that I won’t try to name because naming it would make it smaller.

The words emerged from her mind and the environment, through her hands, through the keys, onto paper. An analog act. No screen between the thought and the page. She snapped a photo of it. I recorded her reading it. But the poem was born on paper, and that’s where it lives. The creation was sovereign even if echoes of it exist elsewhere.

You’ll have to trust me. It was everything.

I’ve been thinking about languages. Not Spanish and English, though both were present that day. Something else.

There’s the language we speak. Words, grammar, meaning carried in sentences. It’s powerful but it divides as a condition of functioning. Subject, object. Us, them. To name something is to separate it from everything it’s not.

There’s the language we build. Code, protocols, algorithms, systems. It carries logic. It optimizes. It scales. But it strips context the way a straightened river strips a bosque. Fast, efficient, and missing everything that lived in the eddies.

And there’s a third language. One that lives underneath the first two. Not in the words. Not in the logic. In the space between people when they’re actually present to each other. It doesn’t belong to either speaker. It emerges between them. Babies speak it before they speak anything else. Animals speak it. Music speaks it.

You can’t write in the third language. You can only create environments where it becomes audible again.

A carpenter’s apprentice doesn’t learn to work with wood from a textbook. They learn it by standing next to someone whose hand slows when the grain is about to split. Nobody explains it. The hand slows. After enough times, the apprentice’s hand slows too. The knowledge transfers through presence, not instruction. That’s why apprenticeship can’t be digitized. You can’t stream proximity. You have to be in the room.

There’s an old distinction in hospitality: customer service is what you receive. Hospitality is how you feel. Service is the first language. Correct, efficient, measurable. Did you get your coffee in sixty seconds? Hospitality is the third. Present, unrepeatable, felt. Sara wasn’t providing customer service. She asked “what is the theme.” She absorbed a stranger’s weight into her work. That’s not a transaction. That’s hospitality.

Her sign said it before I arrived: *I translate your heart.* Not your words. Your heart. She didn’t need grammar for that. She needed a table, a street, and the nerve to be present.

This is what the straightening costs us. I wrote about it recently, about what happens when you optimize the channel for throughput and efficiency. The river moves faster. The water arrives on time. But the bosque dies. The eddies disappear. The minnow can’t reproduce. When we straighten the channel of human attention, optimizing for engagement, for speed, for delivery of stimulus to response, we don’t just lose patience or depth. We lose the conditions for emergence. The environment where the third language can be heard.

The apprentice never stands next to the master long enough. The hospitality becomes service. The presence becomes content. The typewriter becomes a keyboard and the keyboard becomes a prompt and the prompt becomes an output and nobody’s hand is on their heart anymore.

I know the irony. This is digital content about an analog moment. You’re reading it on a screen. An algorithm may have surfaced it. The third language requires presence and I’m asking you to find it in a medium (lowercase m) designed for absence. I can’t give you the cobblestone’s footsteps or the coin getting louder in the cup or Sara’s hand on her heart. I can only tell you they happened and that they changed me and hope that something crosses the gap between my remembering and your reading. That gap is real. I won’t pretend it isn’t.

I walked Calle del Arenal back toward the plaza after. Everything had changed and nothing had. I was lighter.

Here is what I think happened on that sidewalk, and I want to be precise about it because I think it matters beyond me. Three strangers stood in a five-foot radius on a street in Madrid. None of them shared a language. None of them shared a circumstance. One needed food. One offered poetry. One was looking for a gift shop. And for a few minutes, something emerged between them that none of them produced alone. That emergence, whatever it is, is the thing I’ve been trying to build toward in my work for twenty-three years. It’s the thing I’ve been writing about in these essays. It’s the thing our systems are optimized to prevent. We live in a world that hasn’t prioritized having less trauma. It has prioritized having more throughput. The plaza prioritized neither. It just held the space. And what emerged was enough.

Madrid in February. Late afternoon sun hitting the upper floors while the street stayed cool. Tall buildings on both sides, stone facades, and the sound of the city bouncing between them. The cobblestone ground made everything echo. The voices, the footsteps, the laughter all bounced off the facades and came back softer. The people absorbed sound too. Thousands of them, moving through the plaza, and it was quiet in the way a forest is quiet. Not silent. Full of life. But no single sound dominating. No engines. No screens. Stone, voices, and the bodies that absorb both sound and silence.

A string quartet had set up at the edge of the plaza. Four musicians, playing something classical that I couldn’t name but my body recognized. On the far side, near a roundabout off the plaza by a flower stand, a larger group. Six musicians. An orchestra in miniature. They were playing covers, classics, and whatever lives in between. The two groups weren’t coordinated. They weren’t performing together. But they weren’t competing either. Each group had its own gravity.

I watched what happened when people walked near them. The music didn’t stop anyone. It slowed them. A couple adjusting their pace without deciding to. A family pausing, the children turning toward the sound before the parents noticed. A man standing alone with his eyes closed. The music was not a wall. It was a field. You entered it and your body responded before your mind caught up.

The third language again. The strings weren’t speaking Spanish or English. They were speaking in vibration, resonance, interval. The crowd responded in their bodies before their minds could categorize it.

The entire plaza was in motion, thousands of people, dozens of first languages, and it worked. Not despite the diversity. Because of it. The buskers and the talkers and the walkers and the children and the man with the cup and the woman with the typewriter back on Calle del Arenal. All of it. Held in the same space. None of it forced into alignment or arrangement.

Harmony.

I want to be careful with this word.

It’s not agreement. It’s not unison. It’s not the same note played louder. Harmony is emergent. It’s what arises when different things are held in the same environment and the environment has enough gravity to let them coexist without making them the same.

Nobody in that plaza chose to harmonize. The cobblestone reflected sound. The bodies absorbed it. The strings offered gravity. The crowd self-organized around conditions none of them controlled. Harmony wasn’t conducted. It emerged. The plaza created the conditions and the rest happened on its own.

Some human languages have more windows into the third than others. Japanese has ma, the space between things treated as a thing itself. Potawatomi, which the botanist Robin Wall Kimmerer writes about, is so verb-rich that a bay is not a noun but a process. *To be a bay.* The world isn’t made of objects. It’s made of happenings. Quechua requires you to encode how you know what you’re claiming, built into the grammar of every sentence. Spanish has *duende*, what Lorca called the dark mysterious power that rises through the soles of the feet when art becomes something beyond cognitive technique.

These aren’t the third language. They’re the first language pointing at it. Windows, not the view. But some windows are wider than others. And we’ve been boarding them up. Yesterday, Sara didn’t need any of them. She needed a typewriter, a sidewalk, and a stranger willing to answer honestly.

The second language, code and systems, tried to replace the third by making the first more efficient. Optimize the words. Accelerate the delivery. Straighten the channel. And in doing so it paved over the thing that was never in the words to begin with. What’s missing isn’t better words or better code. What’s missing is the environment where the third language can be heard. The Very Large Array’s radio-quiet zone. The plaza. The eddy in the bosque.

I think about the beggar, Sara, and me. Three people who had nothing in common. A man who needed to eat. A woman who translates hearts for strangers on a sidewalk. A man from New Mexico carrying weeks of forgiveness looking for a gift shop.

For a few minutes on Calle del Arenal, the city collapsed to the three of us. The thousands of people disappeared. Two sounds: a coin against a cup and keys against paper. Both asking for something. Both making something.

I couldn’t feed him. I could only meet his eyes. I still ache.

She gave me what I didn’t know I was carrying. A bigger window.

The poem holds all three of us now. Dated, titled, typewritten on a page I’ll bring home to my wife, who I’ve stood beside for every other poem we’ve collected from poets like Sara in other cities, other sidewalks. This one I found alone. I’ll read it to her the way Sara read it to me after some practice. She’ll understand it the way I did.

The strings are still playing somewhere in Madrid as I write this. The cobblestone is echoing and the crowd is slowing and the music is doing what music does. Not commanding. Inviting. Offering a kind of gravity that doesn’t require understanding. Just presence.

Forgiveness and love. That was the theme I gave her. In English. She wrote it in Spanish. She read it to me in Spanish.

I understood every word.

That’s harmony, too.

When was the last time you let yourself be understood?

I think healing comes next. But that’s another essay. For now, the page is in my backpack. Ink pressed into paper. 200226. El Mundo.
