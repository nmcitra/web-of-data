What Was Growing
On trust, physics, and the largest living organism on Earth

In 1998, a team from the U.S. Forest Service went looking for why trees were dying in eastern Oregon. Conifers across the Blue Mountains, thousands of acres of ponderosa pine and Douglas fir, were collapsing from the inside. Roots rotting. Bark peeling back to reveal something dark and fibrous underneath. The trees looked healthy from a distance. Up close, they were already gone.

What the team found wasn't a disease. It wasn't climate. It wasn't logging damage.

It was a single organism.

Armillaria ostoyae. A honey fungus. It had been growing beneath the Malheur National Forest for approximately 8,000 years. Its mycelium stretched across 2,200 acres, roughly 3.5 square miles of living network threading through the soil, wrapping around roots, digesting and redistributing and connecting. The largest living organism on Earth, and nobody knew it was there until the trees started showing symptoms.

They called it the Humongous Fungus. Scientists confirmed through DNA sequencing that the entire network was one organism. One genetic identity. One life, spread across a landscape, doing its work beneath the surface.

I've been building something for 23 years. I'm starting to see what it is.

---

I don't say that as a boast. I say it because the physics only becomes visible at scale.

In 2003, I followed a friend into a building.

That's it. That's the origin story. My trajectory didn't point toward a major university. It didn't point toward networking or cybersecurity or any of what came after. It pointed toward a community college in Albuquerque, TVI, now CNM, because a trusted friend was going there for business administration, and I followed his exact footsteps through the door.

First week. First class. Introduction to Computers. They were teaching us how to use the mouse. How to drag files.

Something in me said no. Not this. The other thing.

I changed my major to Networking Technology before the second week started. Found the Cisco Networking Academy tucked inside the program like a seed inside a seed. CCNA certification. Subnetting. Spanning Tree Protocol. The kind of foundational work that doesn't make anyone's highlight reel. I was learning how packets move, how routes converge, how networks decide which path to trust.

I didn't know it then, but I was learning the grammar of trust itself.

Spanning Tree Protocol, STP, exists because network loops are fatal. When a packet can travel in circles forever, the network drowns in its own traffic. STP solves this by electing a root bridge, pruning redundant paths, creating a hierarchy that prevents loops while preserving resilience. It's elegant. It's also a governance framework disguised as an engineering protocol.

I grew up on networks. TCP/IP. UDP. Layer 3 routing. Session management. ISAKMP. All that jargon I learned like the back of my hand. Exactly how networks work, at the protocol level, at the packet level, at the layer where electrons become decisions.

Here's what nobody talks about: nothing has changed.

Nothing has changed in asphalt. Nothing has changed in LAN/WAN. Sure, we get new materials, smoother roads from time to time. But I'm talking about the pavement and the painted lines. The way networks work, the fundamental physics of how data moves from here to there, has not changed much in 20 years. What has changed is what's riding on those roads. Ghosts. Agents. Fast.

The point is simple and it's everything: knowing the fundamentals is key. The roads don't change. The traffic changes. And if you don't know the roads, deeply, structurally, in your bones, you can't see what's new about the traffic.

My instructor at TVI, Bob Hennigan, referred me to a firm called Network Architects out of Albuquerque. I got an internship. When it ended, they hired me as a consulting engineer. I didn't know what that was. But I had an amazing team of people who still support me, and I have the privilege of calling them friends. Bob referred me to Rebecca, and that referral took me 10 years into the future.

Every major inflection traces back to a person. A relationship. A referral. A door held open.

Alongside the engineering, there was craft. A friend and mentor named Klaus Mueller taught me something roughly 20 years ago that I'm still metabolizing: the way we communicate written information is just as important as the information itself. Not more important. Not separate from. Just as. The integrity of the information, made of data, is key to insights. And integrity has a form. Clean diagrams that are detailed but simple to understand. Executive narratives that compress without distorting. Beautiful documentation, and I mean beautiful, because beauty in communication isn't vanity. It's trust. When the form has integrity, the reader can receive the content. When the form is broken, the content dies on arrival.

I wrote scope of work documents by hand. Packed with details. Executive Summary, planning phase, execution phase, hand-off, support and ongoing. How much. What parts. Who is doing what, and when. Every SOW was a trust document before I knew trust was what I was studying. A handshake rendered in ink. And I delivered ahead of schedule. Thank goodness for project managers, by the way. They all need a big raise and free coffee. Thank you for your service, PMs. I mean that.

Brené Brown and Adam Grant developed a framework called Strong Ground. The 5 Cs: Context, Color, Connective Tissue, Cost, and Consequence. These aren't rhetorical techniques. They're trust primitives. Context is environment. Color is specificity, the sensory detail that makes information real. Connective Tissue is relationship between parts. Cost is what it takes. Consequence is what happens if you don't. That framework maps almost suspiciously well to what I'd later build into the Context Tensor. Trust communication and trust computation turn out to be the same physics at different clock speeds.

In 2016, I moved to ANM after years at Presidio, who had acquired INX. I carry authentic gratitude for both Presidio and ANM. Their training, their investment, their patience with a mind that wouldn't stop connecting things. These are core contributions to everything that followed. At ANM, I learned Cisco's SAFE framework. Secure Architecture for the Enterprise. A brilliant methodology for mapping end-user flow to application or service, viewed through a security lens. I helped ANM achieve their Masters in Security designation from Cisco, a top-tier recognition. I lived inside that framework deeply enough to see where it stopped.

SAFE focused on security. Only security. But the flow it mapped, human to service, endpoint to application, was bigger than any single lens. So I took one assumption off and put another on. Then another. Then another. The IT Service Blueprint was born: the same flow, the same architecture, but viewed through IT operations, fraud, security, compliance, user experience. Not one lens. All of them. The same river, seen from every bank at once.

From the Blueprint came ODAM, the Operationalizing Data Analytics Methodology. Six years of active development, starting in 2016, published in 2022. ODAM is the methodology for human speed. How analysts think. How teams coordinate. How organizations move from signal to decision when a human being is in the loop.

Since 2020, I've worked at Splunk, now part of Cisco. The circle completing itself. Fighting fraud through identity and behavioral analytics, fighting cyber adversaries, preventing network outages, defending against application and human-layer attacks through collective defense and threat intelligence sharing. The work sharpened everything. Fraud taught me that trust is the attack surface. Identity taught me that context is the defense. Collective defense taught me that no single node survives alone.

Then, in the summer of 2025, I sat down to write a manuscript about machine speed attacks. I thought I was writing a book. The first version drafted cleanly enough. But my mind kept going. Because ODAM was built for human cognition, and the threats had exceeded human cognition. The clock rate had changed. The architecture needed to change with it.

The Kinetic Trust Protocol is what ODAM becomes when the speed exceeds the human. KTP. 26 RFCs, a patent, a framework that treats trust not as policy but as physics. It didn't arrive as a revelation. It arrived the way mycelium arrives. One thread at a time, following nutrients. Following a friend through a door. Following a framework past its own limits.

TVI. Klaus. Handwritten SOWs. Delivering ahead of schedule. The 5 Cs. SAFE. The IT Service Blueprint. ODAM. KTP. Not separate projects. Speciation. Each organism evolved to fill a niche the previous one couldn't reach. Each one carried the DNA of the last. The same physics, running faster, reaching deeper, connecting more.

---

We are in a moment where everyone is building AI governance frameworks, and almost all of them are wrong in the same way.

They're wrong because they're trees.

The EU AI Act is a tree. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is a tree. The alignment research community's taxonomies of risk. Trees. Hierarchical. Static. Rooted in a single trunk of authority that branches outward into increasingly specific rules, each leaf a compliance requirement, each branch a jurisdiction.

Trees are beautiful. Trees are necessary. But trees are not how forests work.

Forests work because of what's underneath.

The Wood Wide Web.

That's what scientists call the mycorrhizal network that connects root systems across species, across distance, across the apparent boundaries between individual organisms. It's a fantastic name because it's precisely what it is. A Douglas fir shares carbon with a paper birch through fungal intermediaries neither tree controls. A mother tree redistributes resources to struggling seedlings through channels that exist in no org chart. The forest's intelligence is not in any single tree. It's in the network. It's in the soil. It's in the mycelium.

This is what I've been building. Not a tree. Not a policy framework. Not a compliance taxonomy. A substrate.

---

The Kinetic Trust Protocol begins with a single axiom:

A ≤ E

Autonomy cannot exceed environment.

This is not a rule. Rules can be broken, waived, amended, lobbied against. This is a constraint in the way gravity is a constraint. You don't comply with gravity. You don't audit your gravitational posture quarterly. Gravity is the condition in which everything else happens.

A ≤ E says: the degree of freedom any agent can exercise is bounded by the environment's capacity to absorb the consequences of that freedom. An AI model cannot be more autonomous than the system monitoring it can understand. A corporation cannot extract more than the community sustaining it can survive. A child cannot range further than the home holding them can reach.

This isn't philosophy. It's physics. And the difference matters.

Philosophy gives you positions to argue. Physics gives you ground to stand on. Every AI safety debate I've watched for the past decade has been philosophical. Should we align AI to human values? Whose values? How do we define harm? These are real questions. They're also the wrong altitude. They're canopy questions. They assume the trees are the system.

The mycelium doesn't care about your position on alignment. It negotiates trust at every node, every connection, every moment, based on local conditions and systemic capacity. The forest that emerges from healthy mycelium doesn't need a policy document. It needs physics.

---

Alfred North Whitehead saw this a century ago, though he was looking at a different forest.

Whitehead's process philosophy rejected the idea that reality is made of things, static substances sitting in fixed relationships. Reality, he argued, is made of occasions of experience. Events, not objects. Processes, not positions. The universe doesn't have a state. The universe is a state, continuously becoming the next state.

t → t+1. The universe computes forward.

Trust, in a Whiteheadian frame, is not a noun. It's not a credential you possess, a score assigned to your profile, a token in your wallet. Trust is a verb. An ongoing event that must be renewed at every occasion or it ceases to exist. The moment you treat trust as a thing you have, you've already lost it. Trust is always trusting. Always in motion. Always kinetic.

This is why I named it what I named it. The Kinetic Trust Protocol. Not because it sounds good, though it does, but because kinetic is the precise word. Trust that stops moving is not trust. It's reputation. It's credential. It's a photograph of something that used to be alive.

The mycelium under the Blue Mountains has been renegotiating its relationship with every root system it touches for 8,000 years. Not once. Not annually. Continuously. Every chemical signal, every nutrient exchange, every thread extending or retracting. That's trust in process. Whitehead would recognize it immediately.

---

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari would recognize it too, though they'd use different language.

In A Thousand Plateaus, they distinguished between two kinds of structures: the arborescent and the rhizomatic. The arborescent is the tree. Hierarchical, centralized, traceable to a single root. The rhizomatic is the network. Lateral, decentralized, capable of connecting any point to any other point without passing through a center.

Every governance framework for AI that exists today is arborescent. Every one. Regulatory bodies sit at the root. Standards branch from regulations. Compliance requirements branch from standards. Audit mechanisms branch from requirements. And at the very tips, the leaves, individual organizations try to prove they're attached to the right branch.

The rhizome doesn't work this way. It has no root. No trunk. No privileged center. It spreads laterally, makes connections opportunistically, and can be broken at any point without killing the network. Cut a tree's trunk and the canopy dies. Cut a rhizome anywhere and it routes around the damage.

This is not an argument against governance. This is an argument about what kind of structure can actually govern complex adaptive systems. Trees govern forests the way org charts govern companies. On paper. The actual governance happens underground, in the relationships, in the lateral connections, in the trust negotiations that no hierarchy authorized.

Deleuze and Guattari theorized the rhizome. They wrote brilliantly about it. But they wrote it as philosophy.

I wrote it as RFCs.

Twenty-six of them. Each one a specification for how trust negotiation happens at the substrate level. Not as policy imposed from above, but as physics emerging from below. The Context Tensor, with its seven dimensions including the Soul dimension for Indigenous data sovereignty. The trust handshake protocols. The autonomy calibration mechanisms. The forgiveness functions.

The difference between a philosopher describing the rhizome and an engineer building one is the difference between a mycologist studying the Humongous Fungus and the fungus itself.

One observes. The other grows.

---

There is an older wisdom here, older than Whitehead, older than Deleuze, older than the Western philosophical tradition entirely.

A person is a person through other persons.

This is not a proverb. It's an ontological claim. Identity is not something you possess prior to relationship. Identity is constituted by relationship. You do not exist first and then connect. You connect, and from connection, existence emerges.

The entire Western AI safety conversation assumes the opposite. It assumes autonomous agents, discrete, bounded, self-contained, that must be constrained from the outside. Alignment is the project of taking a thing that exists independently and pointing it in the right direction. Guardrails. Boundaries. Leashes.

But there is no agent without relation. Autonomy doesn't precede environment. Environment produces autonomy. A ≤ E is not a leash on a free agent. It's a description of how agency comes into being in the first place.

The mycelium knows this. No individual thread of the Humongous Fungus has an identity apart from the network. The organism is the network. Cut a thread and it's nothing. A filament of dead tissue. Connected, it's part of the largest living thing on the planet.

We keep building AI governance as if the agents are the primary reality and the connections are secondary. The mycelium says it's the other way around. The connections are primary. The agents are what emerge when the connections are healthy.

---

So what emerges?

This is the question I've been circling for 23 years, and only recently has the answer started to surface. Not as theory. As observation.

Because something is growing.

On January 13, 2026, I drove three hours north from Las Cruces before dawn to have dinner with my brother. It was his birthday celebration. Our whole family at one table for the first time in over a decade. I'll say more about why in the next essay. For now: it was a lovely dinner. It was a table that almost didn't exist.

I thought about Noma on the drive up. By the time I was driving south through the night, back down I-25 toward home, the entire architecture had formed. A proximity-based trust network, built on KTP, built on the physics, that measures and maps the trust relationships people actually have, not the ones institutions assign them. Noma was born on the same road, the same day, as a family reunion that the system said shouldn't happen.

The drive keeps teaching me things.

ChileSec, 300+ members in the cybersecurity community, was born on the same highway, different year. I'd been in Santa Fe for a small regional gathering of state IT directors. At the time, I had developed ODAM and I knew something about it that most framework builders miss: a methodology without local soil is just a document. ODAM needed a network to root in before it could spread. I drove south through the desert toward Las Cruces, and somewhere between Albuquerque and home, the community formed. Not a chapter of someone else's organization. Not a branch office. A local network, built on purpose, so that practitioners in New Mexico could align as a community first. Rhizomatic. Lateral. The kind of structure that no hierarchical conference could produce from the top down.

The Risk-Based Alerting Community, over 1,000 practitioners across the globe, started because Elizabeth Schaedler said one sentence. We'd finished an internal enablement session, the kind of training that usually stays inside the walls. Elizabeth looked at what we'd built and said: "We should do global enablement." One sentence. One person seeing further than the original scope. That's the mycorrhizal signal. The network telling you where to grow next. I didn't plan a global community. Someone at a single node said more, and I followed the nutrient.

The Fraud ATT&CK Matrix, 434 detections, has prevented over $8 billion in losses across the organizations that adopted it. Another fruiting body. Different soil. Same mycelium.

For years, I looked at these as separate projects. Separate efforts. Separate branches of a career that didn't have a single trunk.

I was wrong.

They're fruiting bodies. They're the honey mushrooms pushing through the soil of the Blue Mountains. Different locations, different shapes, different functions, but one organism underneath. One set of physics. One mycelium.

And every single one traces back to a relationship. A friend walking into TVI. A family dinner. A desert highway. A sentence from a colleague. The mycelium didn't grow from a plan. It grew from connection.

The Humongous Fungus wasn't discovered because someone went looking for a fungus. It was discovered because the symptoms were visible. Trees dying, bark peeling, roots dissolving. The organism itself had always been there. It was doing its work beneath the surface, and the only evidence of its existence was what it produced where people could see.

The Tao Te Ching says: The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao.

Chapter 11 says something more practical:

Thirty spokes share the wheel's hub; It is the center hole that makes it useful. Shape clay into a vessel; It is the space within that makes it useful. Cut doors and windows for a room; It is the holes which make it useful. Therefore profit comes from what is there; Usefulness comes from what is not there.

I've spent 23 years building spokes. The physics, the protocols, the communities, the applications, the frameworks, the detections. Thirty spokes, more or less. Each one real. Each one functional. Each one pointed at the same center.

The center is empty. That's what makes it work.

I can't name what's at the center of this work any more than the mycelium can name what drives it through the soil. It's not ambition. It's not career strategy. It's not even purpose, in the way that word usually gets used. Purpose implies a destination, and this isn't going somewhere. It's growing. The way the Humongous Fungus grows. Not toward a goal. Toward nutrients. Toward connection. Toward the next root system that needs what it carries.

---

Here is what I know, 23 years into the growing:

The universe computes forward. t → t+1. You don't get to go back and plant the seed differently. You don't get to choose the soil, the climate, the season. You get the conditions you get, and you grow or you don't.

Consciousness is trajectory. Not a state. Not a position. A direction through the space of what's possible, constrained by what's actual.

Freedom lives inside constraint. Not despite it. Inside it. The river doesn't resent its banks. The banks are why the river has force. A ≤ E is not a limitation. It's the condition that makes power possible.

Physics survives what policy doesn't. Seeds survive fire. The Humongous Fungus survived 8,000 years of surface-level catastrophe. Ice, drought, logging, wildfire. It lives below the layer where catastrophe operates. The policy frameworks we're building for AI today will be obsolete in a decade. The physics won't.

And something is growing. More than one seed has sprouted. The forest floor is filling in. I can see the canopy from here, not complete, not yet, but forming. The connections between the fruiting bodies are becoming visible. The mycelium is surfacing.

Not because I willed it. Because the conditions permit it. Because the soil is ready. Because 8,000 years, or 23, is how long it takes for an organism this size to reach maturity.

---

What the forest looks like from here, what's emerging, what it costs, and what it asks of us. That's the next orbit. The closer pass.

For now: the fungus is real. The network is alive. And the trees that are dying, the policies, the frameworks, the arborescent governance structures that can't hold the weight of what's coming, they're not the tragedy.

They're the signal.

Something underneath has reached the surface.
