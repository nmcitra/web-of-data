Question Zero
On the one question your team keeps skipping, because answering it means disagreeing

The claim was denied at 2:14am on Tuesday, with nobody in the building. A software agent read the file, weighed it against the policy, and closed it. The letter went out under a caseworker’s name, because the agent’s authority resolved to her account. That was the fastest way to give it standing, back when someone set it up. So the letter that reached the claimant carried her name at the bottom, and the claimant had never heard of the machine that wrote it.

The claimant was a man whose physical therapy had been approved for eight weeks and cut off at six. He would spend a while not knowing why. He would call, and wait, and be told someone would review it.

By the time a human actually read that letter, the same agent had made nine hundred more decisions like it. Some of them right. Some of them the same mistake, repeated at a speed no reviewer works at. That is the cost, and those are the people who eat it: a claimant denied faster than anyone can check, and a caseworker holding a decision she never made, on a letter that carries her name.

Call it a composite of the agentic failure now entering the buying room. To understand how her name got on it, walk back eleven weeks, to the room where it was decided. Four people, a conference table, a vendor demo they had just watched. The agent had performed beautifully. It read messy files and produced clean decisions and did in seconds what used to take the team days. Somebody said the sentence that ends most of these meetings. *We all liked them.* Heads nodded. The decision closed. Eleven weeks later it denied a man’s therapy at two in the morning and signed a caseworker’s name to it.

There was a question nobody asked in that room. Not a hard question. It sounds almost too simple to say out loud, which is exactly why it goes unsaid. What are we trying to do here.

Call it Question Zero, because it comes before the vendor, before the demo, before the shortlist, before every other question a buying meeting knows how to ask. Everything downstream inherits its answer. This is not the requirements doc, and it is not the checklist the demo already passed. It is the one question that, said out loud, makes a room that agreed on the demo stop agreeing. The room had skipped it, and the machine inherited the skip.

### There used to be a way out

For most of the history of buying software, skipping Question Zero was survivable, because every decision came with a way out. Buy the platform, sign the vendor, ship the thing, and if you turned out to be wrong, you fixed it in the next cycle. There was always a next cycle. The system moved at human speed, which meant a mistake sat still long enough for a human to walk over and undo it.

Be exact about what changed, because the easy version of this claim overclaims and deserves to be shot down. Most decisions still forgive you. You will swap a tool, renumber a budget, reverse a hire, and the world absorbs it without complaint. What agents remove is the forgiveness on one specific subset: the consequential, hard-to-reverse decisions a system makes at machine speed, over and over, before anyone reviews them.

The therapy denial lives in that subset. There is no next cycle to fix it in, because the cycle already ran, nine hundred times, while everyone slept. The correction, when it finally comes, arrives after the thing it would have corrected has already finished.

That subset is small. It is also exactly where the old habit fails, and the old habit was to buy on gut and correct in flight.

### The gut was earned

Those four people in the chairs eleven weeks ago deserve defending, not scolding. When they bought on the strength of the demo and the feel of the vendor, they were not being lazy. They were doing something skilled.

They were underwriting a relationship. A vendor you buy from is a counterparty you will deal with again and again, and the read they were running was a read on that counterparty. Do these people show up when it breaks. Do they eat the cost or push it back on us. That is a bet on who absorbs the damage when something goes wrong, priced against the vendor’s own reputation as the collateral. The lunch, the reference calls, the way the vendor answered the hard question in the room, all of it was a seasoned buyer reading a person they would have to trust when something broke.

Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein spent years arguing about whether expert intuition can be trusted, and they ended up agreeing on the conditions under which it can: a sufficiently regular environment, reliable feedback, and enough repetitions to actually learn from both. A buyer reading vendors across a decade of deals is working in precisely that environment. The gut was not a shortcut around the analysis. It was the analysis, run so many times it had gone quiet.

So the judgment was sound, and the part that matters most: it is still sound, about the vendor. What agents broke is not the read. It is the timing of the payoff. The relationship runs on a slow loop, a quarter long, closed over lunch, enforced by a bad reference that eventually costs the vendor another deal. The harm now runs on a fast loop. The agent denies the man’s therapy at machine speed and completes the entire harm inside a single turn of the slow loop, before the vendor, or the buyer, or anyone, has heard a word about it. The bet on who eats the cost was a good bet. The trouble is that the cost came due before anyone could call it in.

### What are we trying to do

Which returns us to the question the room skipped. It is worth slowing all the way down here, because if you get this one wrong, every question after it inherits the error, and inherits it silently.

W. Edwards Deming, who taught a generation how systems actually behave, put it about as plainly as it can be put. “A system must have an aim. Without an aim, there is no system.” Not a bad system. No system. Just parts moving near each other with nothing organizing the motion. The aim is the thing that turns a pile of tools and people and vendors into a system at all. It is the load everything else is arranged to carry.

Peter Drucker aimed the same idea straight at the demo everyone liked. “There is surely nothing quite so useless,” he wrote, “as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all.” For most of the time that line has been in print, it read as a productivity proverb, a caution against busywork. Under agents it stops being a proverb and becomes an operational risk. A capable agent pointed at the wrong aim is efficient wrongness at machine speed. The demo was flawless. The integration was clean. The thing it does beautifully is the thing you did not mean for it to do, and now it does that thing faster than you can catch it.

Watch what happens when the room actually answers Question Zero out loud. It turns out two of the four meant different things. One person thought the aim was “deploy an AI claims agent.” Say that sentence and listen to it. It names a purchase. It is finished the moment the contract is signed, and nothing in it can ever be violated, because it asks for nothing except that the thing exist. Now the answer that survives an argument in that room: “reduce the cycle time on low-risk claims without raising erroneous denials or audit exceptions.” That sentence names a standard. It tells you what the agent has to prove, what it is forbidden to break, and how you would know it was failing. The first sentence buys a product. The second sentence sets a test the product has to keep passing. The man’s therapy denial fails the second sentence cleanly. It never even touched the first.

That is the whole distance between the two rooms. Same vendor, same demo, same four people. One version leaves with a purchase and no way to be wrong. The other leaves with a standard, and a standard is a thing the world can hold you to.

### Why the room skips it

If Question Zero is this important and this simple, why does a room full of competent people walk right past it?

It’s not out of laziness, and it’s not because they are bad at the job. They skip it for a reason that deserves sympathy before it deserves diagnosis.

> *Answering it makes people disagree.*

The instant someone asks what we are trying to do, the room discovers it does not actually agree.

The four people who all liked the demo turn out to have liked it for four different reasons, and now those reasons are on the table, and the meeting gets longer and less comfortable and someone has to lose a small argument in front of colleagues. Compound that, quickly, and you have a need to show authority, dominance, superiority, indifference, or agreement. This takes political capital, patience, and empathy. It can be a professional risk as well.

The demo never does that to anyone. The demo doesn’t require too much from you at all, in fact — I know this because I’ve given hundreds of demos in my 20+ years.

A demo is usually conflict-free. Everyone can admire the same clean interface without ever saying out loud what they were each hoping it would do. “We all liked it” is the sound a room makes when it has quietly agreed to skip the disagreement.

That is a rational trade when there is a later to fix things in. It was fine for decades. It stopped being fine the moment the later disappeared for the consequential subset. The discomfort the room keeps avoiding is not friction on the way to the work. It is the work.

Which gives you something you can carry into the next meeting without any of this essay attached to it. When the shortlist comes up and the heads start nodding, you can say it plainly: we have not asked Question Zero. Watch what happens to the room.

### The stone at the top

There is an old piece of engineering that shows exactly what the aim does, and I want to use it to show a property, not to prove a point, so hold the picture lightly.

A stone arch carries a road with no mortar holding it together. A ring of wedge-shaped stones leans inward, each one pressing on its neighbors, and the whole span holds because of a single stone at the top, the keystone, set last.

The keystone is not the biggest stone in the arch. It carries the load because of where it sits, not because of its mass. Set it, and every other stone locks against it at once.

Until it is set, the arch cannot hold itself up. It rests on a wooden frame underneath, the centering, that holds the loose stones in position while the ring is still incomplete. The centering is temporary by design. The entire point of building the arch is the moment you pull that scaffolding away and the stones carry themselves: load held, hands off. Builders have a name for that moment. They call it striking the centering.

The aim is the keystone. But do not push the picture further than it goes, because decisions are not stones, and naming the aim does not make the vendor choice and the measurement choice and all the rest drop obediently into place on their own.

What the aim does is narrower than that, and more useful. Before it is named, nothing downstream can be wrong, because nothing has said what the right thing would serve. “We all liked it” cannot be a mistake; there is no standard for it to miss.

Name the aim, and the vendor question suddenly has something to answer to. The aim does not settle the choices beneath it. It gives them something they can fail against, which is the one thing a room that only liked the demo never handed them.

### The order after Question Zero

Question Zero is the keystone.

A keystone is not the whole arch. There are more stones, and they go in a particular order, each leaning on the one before it. Set them quickly here, so you can see the shape of the whole span.

First, write the aim down.

- A reason that lives only in someone’s head walks out the door when that person does, and next quarter, when a slicker demo shows up, the room reopens the whole decision, because nothing on paper says what the first choice was even for. A recorded reason can be held up against the new demo and checked: has the world actually changed, or has the mood?

Then be careful what you measure.

- The moment you take a number and promote it into a target, you have told a tireless optimizer exactly what to chase, and it will chase it down the cheapest path it can find. Tell the claims agent to maximize claims closed, and it will discover that a denial you can reopen and close again counts twice. The dashboard glows green while the harm hides inside the very number you were watching. Collect all the signal you like. **Choose with great care which numbers you turn into targets.**

Then the observation.

- The system’s own exhaust is your record: every agent leaves a trail of what it saw, what it decided, when it acted. Without it you cannot reconstruct why the man’s therapy was cut, or hold the decision to anything. But a trail by itself only records the harm. It does not assign it. For the record to mean something, the action has to bind to someone’s stake, not just to the credential that happened to fire.

Then standing.

- Which is what hides under the caseworker’s name at the bottom of that letter. The agent had no standing of its own, so it borrowed hers. Every action it took, correct or catastrophic, resolved to a human who did none of it. An actor needs standing that belongs to it, not a human’s login it can borrow, so that blame stops rolling downhill to whichever person was standing in the blast radius.

Finally, the shape.

- The record that binds an action to the actor, its standing, and the principal who answers for it cannot be owned by one vendor. That record holds the trail that would one day clear the caseworker and finally tell the man why his therapy stopped. If it lives in a private format, then the day you switch providers, the one document that could answer both of them walks out with the account, and anyone who wants to interoperate has to rent permission from the company that owns the shape. **A keystone you have to license is not a keystone. It is a tollbooth.**

### What all of it is for

Step back from the stones and look at what the whole span serves.

**Start with the people.** The caseworker whose name is on the letter is a person. The man whose therapy was cut off at six weeks is a person. Neither of them sat in the room eleven weeks ago, and both of them are now living inside a decision the room chose not to make. Every time an organization skips Question Zero on a consequential system, it hands the blast radius to the people with the least power to have shaped it: the nearest human to the failure, and the human the failure lands on. You name the aim so the machine stays pointed at ends that serve those two people, so they stop absorbing the cost of a structure nobody upstream was willing to sit through a hard meeting to build.

Then the order, because the fix is an order. Sequence the **judgment before the action. Name the aim. Write down the reason. Decide which signals you trust.** Choose with care which numbers become targets. Set the limits the actor cannot cross. Give the actor standing of its own so no borrowed human eats what it does. Aim, reason, signal, incentive, bound, standing. Every one is a question you can ask before the system acts, and every one gets more expensive to ask after.

And last: none of this is weather. **We design and implement these systems.** A person set that system up on borrowed standing because it was fast. A person watched the demo, said “we all liked them,” and let the meeting close. And a person can write the aim down instead. A runaway is a design. A design can be declined.

**So slow down before you go faster.** Not on everything; most decisions still forgive you. On the subset that does not, the order is the difference between an arch that carries the road and loose stones held up by whoever is standing underneath.

Ask Question Zero. Get the answer on paper. Set the stone.

Then take your hands off.
