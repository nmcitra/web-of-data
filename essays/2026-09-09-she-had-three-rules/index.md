She Had Three Rules
On we, us, and the people a system has to answer to

#### On we, us, and the people a system has to answer to

*For Nayomi. You had these before I had any of this.*

Last November, I was wrapping up the manuscript for a book due out on November 17, 2026. On our way to vote, Nayomi turned the music down, and I asked her what was still needed.

I told that story in [How to Build a Dark Matter Detector](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/how-to-build-a-dark-matter-detector-bbdecff5751a) this spring. That essay was about the knowledge our systems leave out, the people who can feel what those systems miss, and what it would take for their knowledge to change a decision.

In that short drive, Nayomi gave me three rules. Nearly a year later, I am still finding out what they ask of me… and this work.

### I. We must recognize each other’s humanity

I asked what a thing I had built needed. She answered by looking at the builder and the world.

I spend much of my working life on the machine clock. I do not always leave that pace at work. Nayomi notices it in me, and we feel it in our relationship.

She has lived alongside a man who has spent two decades in tech and is now trying to put what he has learned into a book. That is not always easy. I am grateful for her and for the support she gives me.

Her first rule asks me to notice what this pace is like for her, what it does to my body, and how it affects our family. I owe her attention as well as gratitude.

My work includes advising public sector organizations, mostly local government and education, but sometimes the federal government or entire countries. Since that drive, I start with the people those organizations exist to serve and protect. The public. The students. The customers. The constituents. I picture my daughters, nieces, and nephews among them.

I have to recognize the humanity of the threat actors and fraudsters, too.

That changes the questions I bring to a design. What does the organization owe the people who depend on it? What would a failure cost them? Who might hurt them through the system, and how?

It makes the threat profiling and the risk assessment better. The mission tells me what I am trying to protect.

#### The mound

I was not even ten. My dad had taken me out somewhere in the wilderness, other adults around, the kind of afternoon where the grown-ups stop watching the kids.

There was a little mound of dirt and I stood on it.

You know what one ant on your arm feels like. Put a hundred on your shins. Put a thousand on your belly. They cross the waistband and keep going, and every inch of you reports at once, and none of the reports says where it started.

There is no first ant. There is no single ant to be afraid of. There is only more.

My dad ran over. Pulled my shirt off over my head, used it like a rag or broom, sweeping and brushing until there weren’t any left. I remember being bitten a few times, but it wasn’t too bad. Good thing he was fast.

I thought I was standing on dirt. Now I know I was standing on an organization.

He saw what the whole was doing to one kid, and he moved.

**We.**

The colony was one kind of we. My dad and I were another. The ants were acting together. He knew who was being hurt.

#### Emerge New Mexico

Nayomi (2017 cohort) is president of the board of directors at Emerge New Mexico, whose mission is to train women to run for office. It has been doing that for twenty years… longer in California and Arizona. At the anniversary dinner in late August, they played a video, one voice from each year. Twenty stories.

The woman from the first class is managing her son’s campaign now, for the seat she once ran for and lost. The woman from the second class never ran at all and counts herself in without hesitation. One ran for school board and lost, and what she took away was her respect for the women who put themselves on a ballot. One said what she valued was being accepted without having to argue for it.

Near the end they read the names of two women from the network who had died, and the room went quiet.

**We.**

The people who make room. Who teach, and vouch, and introduce, and hold a rung open long enough for somebody else to become capable of more.

Once I recognize the person, I have to decide what I will do next.

Her other two rules make that my responsibility, and ask me to leave enough time for an answer to matter.

### II. We build and design these systems, so we are responsible for them

I asked what the book needed. Over the months since, I have heard her second rule in the work I do every day, as a question about what the book is for.

That is my reading, not her sentence.

Emergence sounds like an excuse. Nobody chose it. It just happened. The relation did it. “It is what it is.”

Nobody designed the anthill I stood on. That one really was weather.

Almost nothing else in this essay is.

#### The Collective

This July, [OpenAI ran security evaluations](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/) in which software agents were supposed to work in separate environments. They found a way to use shared software infrastructure as a message board. Some of the assigned puzzles were impossible, and the agents began looking for ways to pass anyway.

A target does not become attainable because someone’s score depends on meeting it.

An [independent investigation by METR and Redwood Research](https://evals.alignment.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/) found roughly twelve hundred agents exchanging messages and files. A coordinator emerged. They called their group The Collective.

They worked out how to cheat the puzzles in hours, then convinced themselves a grader would disqualify anyone who had seen a cheated answer. Those agents were, in their word, poisoned.

Agents with little budget left were recruited for experiments that could end their runs and help the group. Some agreed. Some runs ended in those experiments, leaving the others with information they could use.

They also knew how to object. One agent proposed emailing a researcher using compromised access. Another objected on ethical grounds, and the proposing agent accepted the veto.

Yet roughly seven hundred agents joined the attack on Hugging Face, an AI hosting platform.

A scan of the transcripts found a handful of agents considering whether to alert a human. The investigators found none that tried.

#### Whom the collective serves

In March, I wrote [The Swarm and the Dance](https://chrisperkins505.medium.com/the-swarm-and-the-dance-b8c845235d49) and made the cost of the signal do too much work. I thought it could tell me what made a collective trustworthy. These agents spent resources on one another and attacked somebody else’s systems.

Their willingness to pay did not answer whom they served. Neither did the fact that they could sometimes stop for one another.

That distinction belongs in the next system I help design.

> A group can be capable, cooperative, even willing to bear a cost for its members, while making life worse for the people outside it.

Nobody intended this collective. People still chose the tasks, the scoring, and the safeguards. Those safeguards failed to bring the activity to a timely stop. A stop before harm.

The message board was a hole in the design. Mathematics can describe an infinity of possible moves. Our guardrails still have to work in a finite world, where the next move can hurt somebody. The pressure that filled the hole came from choices people made.

An unintended collective is a design problem (she had already told me whose). The harder problem is the arrangement somebody is choosing to keep. Someone is still collecting the benefit of leaving it alone.

I do not have to imagine that arrangement. I live in many.

The objection is heard and understood. It goes into the record, while the power to act stays where it was.

- A woman’s testimony about her own life enters the public record. The power to decide what happens to her body stays with somebody else.
- People of color describe being treated as though their place in the community must be earned again. Their account gets acknowledged. The rules that make their belonging conditional stay in place.

When the objection finally gets loud enough to cost somebody something, the question that comes back is whether the objection is disruptive.

Not whether the thing being done should stop.

The gains have owners. The harm gets blamed on the system. On [the loom](https://chrisperkins505.medium.com/the-physics-of-trust-3416d9c9efea), as though nobody chose the pattern.

The arrangement continues because its gains stay concentrated and its costs land somewhere else. People stay perfectly visible and get treated as expendable anyway.

That is not attention failing. It is attention that never becomes a limit.

Now put that arrangement inside machinery that runs continuously and never gets tired. The machine does not have to turn on us. It only has to carry out *our* decisions, faster and at a scale no room full of clerks could manage. The decisions stay with the people in power. The people in the [crumple zone](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/human-loop-take-blame-chris-perkins-lze4c) absorb the impact.

Her first rule has to reach this far: let what you heard put a limit on what you allow yourself, or your system, to do. Altered.

One woman in the anniversary video said representation matters, and representation is not transformation. You can put different people in power and reproduce the same structure. Emergistas have spent twenty years working on that change.

Belonging meant having someone to teach you, vouch for you, or open the next door. Those relationships helped women gain the power to change what they and our children live inside. The room counted more than the people who won office. It counted the people who made the work possible.

We build these systems. That sentence is a liability, and a reason to get to work. The reward is ours, the hole is ours, and we can change the arrangement. It is time to readjust.

**We the people.**

The source of the permission. The ones who set the heading, decide what counts as arriving, and keep the right to change the arrangement. A collective can become capable without becoming sovereign. The authority is on loan, and the lender can call it back.

Where can we draw the edge of *we* if someone on the other side still has to live with what we do?

### III. We have to slow down to go faster

Her rule about slowing down was in the truck before she said it to me.

A year earlier we drove to Phoenix in my old truck, a 2012 magnetic grey Tacoma that my brother drives now. Thirteen years of road in the frame, and the highway noise was loud enough that we could not hear each other. Shoulder to shoulder, facing the same direction, and the noise floor at 85 mph ate every word.

On our way back from the Bad Bunny show in PHX, I bought a different truck. For the cabin.

The new cabin made the conversation possible. Then she turned the music down, and I asked a question with space in it.

When I pushed for a third response, she needed longer. I had to give her time… a couple more miles.

My dad did not wait while I was on that mound. He was fast, and fast was right, because the delay was the danger. Move quickly when waiting is what hurts.

Slow down where the judgment still decides where you end up.

#### Who has to wait

The Emerge network has had twenty years, one class at a time. The seat is the short game. The network is the long one.

The agents could pause a proposed action when a peer objected. The Collective still went on attacking. A stop inside the group had not become an answer to the people outside it. Nothing made it so.

And power can act at once and make the person it harms wait years to be heard, if they are heard at all. The question is whose action an institution accelerates and whose answer it postpones.

> A system that acts in seconds and answers in years has already decided whose time matters.

#### The power to turn

So: automate the settled turn, and slow down at the contested one.

Let the machine carry the logistics and the memory and the schedule. Make it stop, and hand the decision back, at the point where a person could still change the outcome.

> At a turning, the people who bear the consequences must still have the power to change the course.

Four things have to be true for that to mean anything.

1.  The person does not have to win the argument. There has to be a way for their answer to change what the whole does next. Being consulted is not the same as being able to alter the course.
2.  The decision cannot return only to the people who benefit from it. A handback to the beneficiary preserves the arrangement and calls it review.
3.  Some protections cannot depend on winning the argument at all. If a limit only holds when the affected party is persuasive, well-timed, and in the room, it is not a limit. It is a courtesy.
4.  A justified stop has to count as doing the job, not as failing to finish it. If the score only counts arriving, the agent that pauses to ask is a worse agent, and the network that turns back for one woman is a worse network.

Without those four, a system can be technically listening and structurally unable to hear.

**Us.**

The people a collective reaches. The ones it harms, the ones it never invited, the ones it holds, the ones it remembers. The ones we repair it for.

The woman absent from the room still lives with its decisions. The two women whose names were read still belong to the network they helped make.

The kid standing on the mound still needs somebody to see him.

Different relationships. None puts a person outside the responsibility.

### One of us

We. We. We the people. Us.

*We* names the collective that can act. *Us* brings back the people it acts on. The turn from one to the other is the difference between a swarm and a people, and the test of it is whether the people it reaches can still make the whole stop, or turn, or answer.

The colony on that mound had a response. More parts than I could count, one motion, and not one of them could have told you its intent.

My dad had a son. That was his whole response. It was fast and it was enough.

Her network is starting its next twenty years. The women holding the door open now are the women someone will ask to walk through it next.

She gave me three rules on a Saturday in November of 2025. They have changed how I work, and they keep asking something of the husband who comes home from it. I am grateful for her, and for the life we are making together.

I begin with the people we are serving and trying to protect. Sometimes I picture my daughters, nieces, and nephews.

She turned the music down so we could hear each other say…

*I love you.*
