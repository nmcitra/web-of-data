There Is No Field For It


The jazz band from Lynn Middle School played first, after the pledge of allegiance was sung. The country's first, then New Mexico's. In English and Spanish.

Then two elementary art winners came up, one at a time, to stand in front of a room of adults and have their photographs taken. Somebody's grandmother had driven in for this. You could tell by the proud smile. A middle schooler named Marcus Li was recognized for winning the state Civics Bee. He'll be representing New Mexico at the national competition in October.

This is what a school board meeting is before it is anything else.

It was 6pm on a Tuesday night in the Dr. Karen M. Trujillo Administration Complex on South Main, in Las Cruces, and about a hundred people in the room. Five board members who are not paid to be there. A superintendent, Ignacio Ruíz, who is. An agenda that would run until nearly ten.

I went to thank them in public, on the record, and I want to be exact about who. Board President Pamela Cort, Vice President Bob Wofford, Secretary Patrick Nolan, Ed Frank, Teresa Tenorio. Dr. Wendi Miller-Tomlinson, who presented. The parents who came and stayed. The students who played and the students who made speeches about their art.

I was there to use two minutes. I honestly didn't expect to sit captivated for four hours. It was my first real Board of Education meeting for a school district.

### Two minutes (usually it's three)

Las Cruces gives the public a slot near the top of the agenda, before the presentations, which means you speak before you have heard the thing you came to speak about. I used mine on the reading assessment my district gives every kindergarten through second grader, the one that records a child's voice to score how well she reads out loud.

Three asks.

1.  Get written confirmation that the deleted recordings are actually deleted — including whatever was derived from them.
2.  Tell every K-2 family that a paper version exists instead of waiting for families to somehow know to ask.
3.  And carry those protections into the state's next assessment contract, where they would bind whoever wins it.

The board took up the first two that night. That is the whole report on my two minutes, and it is more than I expected.

I stayed for the other three hours and fifty-seven, took three pages of notes, and witnessed a community caring deeply for our kids.

### Four clocks in one room

I have written before about the four clocks that run inside any system with people in it, and that night was the cleanest demonstration I have seen.

- The machine clock. The assessment takes six to nine minutes and returns a fluency score with a decimal in it. Machine clock work, done at machine speed, to machine precision.
- The institutional clock. A state memo, a procurement vehicle, an agenda with roman numerals, a policy manual, a contract cycle that turns over every few years. The two pages I left as a leave-behind. This clock moves in monthly Board meetings and school years, and it is the clock the district actually lives on.
- The human clock. The teacher who has had the kid since August and knows he mumbles when he is embarrassed. The neighbor who came because her friend asked her to. A parent in a folding chair at hour three. A body at a podium. Presence and relationships, which take the time they take.
- The ecological clock. A seven-year-old becoming a reader. Not this year's score, the years. Fast gains early, then a long slow tail, and no way to hurry the middle of it. The slowest thing in the room, and the only one the whole apparatus exists to serve.

Four clocks in one room for four hours. Nothing unusual about that. What is unusual is what each of them left behind.

### The clocks that ship without telemetry

The machine clock emits by construction. That is what it is for. Every session logged, every score stored, every attempt time-stamped, and the vendor can tell you to the decimal how a child in Las Cruces read a passage on a Tuesday in September.

The institutional clock keeps minutes. Agendas, packets, votes, a signature block with five lines in it. Not perfect and not fast, but a record exists, and I can go get it.

The human clock leaves a thin trace. A teacher's note. An email. Something in a file. And almost nothing of what the evening actually cost.

Not the four hours. Not the hundred people who became twenty-five. Not the folding chairs. Not the patience, which was considerable. Not the stress, which was visible. Not what it costs a person to sit through two hours of consent-agenda business to reach the item that has been eating them alive since school started.

And the ecological clock barely moved. Four hours of governing, and the thing being governed, a child turning into a reader, advanced by an amount nobody could see. That clock runs in years. No one in that room could watch the whole of it, and none of the instruments under discussion could hold the whole of it.

And here is the part I keep reflecting on, because I build data systems for a living and this is a data systems observation, not a poem. Those things are not unmeasurable. There is simply no field for them. No column. No schema entry. Nobody ever built the place where that number would go, so the number does not exist, so the thing does not exist to the system, so as far as the system is concerned the cost was never paid.

A schema is a decision about what can be known.

> Every system arrives with a theory of what is worth noticing. Most organizations never inspect that theory because it is hidden inside the schema.

Go look at the last system your organization bought. Somebody chose every field it emits. Somebody also chose every field that it doesn't, and that second list was never written down anywhere.

### What the room cost

I have spoken to rooms of several hundred people without an anxious feeling. It is part of my job. I know the feeling of a big room and I do not fear it.

I had nerves on Tuesday.

I have been trying to work out why ever since — and the closest I can get is this. In a conference room the stakes are professional and reversible. In that room the stakes were my kid's district, my neighbors' kids, and people I have to see at the grocery store. Nothing about it was reversible. My hands knew that before I did.

By hour three the room had thinned to about twenty-five. Nobody there had stopped caring. A four-hour meeting on a school night charges a different price to a parent with a toddler than to a person who is paid to be in the building, and the people still in their chairs at the end are the people who could afford the fare.

The board was still there. They are not paid.

And during public comment a woman spoke ahead of me, fast and bright and urgent, and her arms were shaking. Visibly. She kept going anyway. I do not know her name and this is all I will ever say about her, but I have not stopped thinking about it, because that tremble was the most honest instrument in the building.

Whatever it meant, her body was paying for the moment. Nothing on any dashboard in that district recorded it — and it was one of the clearest signals in the room.

### The questions were better than the answers

Look at what the board was reaching for.

Bob Wofford asked whether anyone knew how often teachers go back and listen to the recordings. He was not the only one circling it. This matters because teacher review is the reason given for keeping a child's voice at all. The answer, in substance, was that the district cannot know. The usage data sits inside the vendor's platform.

A safeguard whose use cannot be inspected is not a safeguard. It is a claim.

Ed Frank asked whether you could learn roughly the same thing by handing a second grader a book at grade level and listening to her read it. Then he apologized for oversimplifying. He should not have. That is the question that comes before every other question, and the room had no slot for it, so the man who asked it assumed he was being naive.

Teresa Tenorio asked what the underlying data was behind a summary of hundreds of community responses that had arrived compressed into a single slide. She was not being difficult. She was being asked to decide from a summary, and she wanted to know what the summary had dropped.

Patrick Nolan said he was not convinced it was working, and then he said the thing that reframed the entire evening for me. It was not the district's choice. The assessment was handed to them and they were told to run it.

None of these people had the four clocks sitting in front of them. All of them were standing on one and asking where its record had gone — Wofford and Frank on the human clock, wanting to know whether a person listening to a child was happening or was even needed, Tenorio on what the institutional clock had compressed on its way to a slide, Nolan on the ceiling the whole room was sitting under.

I brought a vocabulary. They already had the questions. All the vocabulary does is let people find out they were never disagreeing with each other, only standing at different distances from the child. To the Board: I hope this helps you as much as it helps me.

### What gets a field

A child's oral reading fluency: measured to the decimal, stored, transmitted, retained on a schedule, reportable to the state.

What it took to govern that measurement: four hours, a hundred people, five unpaid board members, one woman's shaking hands. No record of any of it.

That gap is not an oversight and it is not anyone's fault in that room. It is what happens when the fast clock builds the instruments. Machine-speed systems arrive with their own telemetry because telemetry is cheap when the thing being measured is already digital. Everything else has to be built on purpose, by someone who decided it was worth building, out of a budget that has to be defended against things that already report numbers.

Prevention is invisible. Patience is invisible. Presence is invisible. They stay invisible until the day they are gone, and then they are the only thing anyone can talk about.

Nolan's sentence is the physics of it. A district can configure a tool, and it cannot choose whether to have the tool. Its room to act is set somewhere upstream, by a contract it did not write. Which means the useful move is not to win the argument about the tool. It is to go change what the next contract requires, while it is still being written.

That is a slower fight and a better one.

### The thing about the jazz band

They played for maybe six to seven minutes. Nobody wrote down what it did to the room. No one counted how many proud parents got to see their child perform for an audience. I was there, paying attention, and I could not tell you now what they played.

I am not asking for these metrics. Demand that a thing produce a number and someone will optimize the number, which is how you kill it. Some things should stay unmeasured on purpose.

But there is a difference between a thing you have chosen not to measure and a thing you have built no capacity to notice. The first is judgment. The second is architecture.

Your systems tell you what they did. What in your building can tell you what it cost, and who would you have to be listening to in order to hear it?

I stand for questions.
