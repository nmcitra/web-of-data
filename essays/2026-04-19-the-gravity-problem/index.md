The Gravity Problem


Imagine a weapon in space.

Jet boosters on every axis. No atmosphere. No friction. No gravity well pulling it anywhere. It can accelerate in any direction at any speed it chooses. Point and go. Nothing resists.

Now put the same weapon on Earth.

Same boosters. Same power. Same intention. But now there's air resistance. Now there's gravity. Every movement costs energy. Every direction has drag. The environment itself is saying no — not through rules, not through commands, but through physics.

The weapon still works. It's still dangerous. But it can't move like it did in the vacuum. The terrain won't allow it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the AI problem, inverted.

We keep asking: How do we make it faster? How do we make it smarter? How do we give it more capability?

The inversion: What constrains it?

Right now, agentic AI operates in vacuum. No friction. No gravity. The only limits are the ones we bolt on afterward — policies, guardrails, terms of service. Rules that depend on the agent agreeing to follow them.

That's not physics. That's a request.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

On Earth, you don't ask the missile to respect gravity. Gravity doesn't care what the missile wants. The constraint holds whether the missile cooperates or not.

That's the difference between policy and physics.

Policy says "please don't."

Physics says "you can't."

------------------------------------------------------------------------

We're building increasingly powerful agents and deploying them in vacuum. No environmental constraint. No terrain that resists. No gravity that holds.

Then we're surprised when they move in directions we didn't anticipate.

The question isn't how to make the weapon listen.

The question is: what does gravity look like for AI?
