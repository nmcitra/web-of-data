The Enclosure of Trust
Jon Stewart’s guests named labor and capital. The missing layer is what lets either of them still function.

About forty-four minutes into [Jon Stewart’s Weekly Show from Earth Day Eve,](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RB_WmoH5nQ4) David Autor is explaining why fair use law never imagined being applied to AI training data. Daron Acemoglu is nodding. And Jon Stewart, sitting between two MIT economists, one of them a Nobel laureate, finds the two-word reframe they needed.

*“It’s reverse socialism,”* he says. *“They’re taking from the workers, and they’re funneling up to these five individuals.”*

The laughter is warm. The point lands. Autor agrees. Acemoglu builds on it. And then the conversation moves to policy, to wage insurance and universal basic capital and tax reform, and I sit there listening on my drive home and feel the familiar thing.

These are smart people. Their work is serious. The policy menu they propose is good.

And yet.

And yet it reads as insufficient to the diagnosis they just delivered. Autor had already said, a few minutes earlier, that *AI is God’s gift to authoritarians.* Acemoglu had already named the ideology of AGI as the thing driving the industry toward bad outcomes. And Sandel’s critique of meritocracy, which Acemoglu called *very, very pernicious,* was hanging over all of it.

The policy menu doesn’t match the scale of the diagnosis. And the reason it doesn’t match is that the three of them, between them, named two of the three forces at work. Labor. Capital. And then stopped.

There is a third force. And without it, the policy menu holds nothing.



------------------------------------------------------------------------



## What they got right

The serious part first, because the serious part deserves credit.

The strongest move Acemoglu makes, and he makes it three times in different vocabularies, is this: the direction of technology is a choice.

He says it as an economist: the direction is highly malleable, not determined by the technology itself but by the incentives set around it. He says it as a warning: there is always a worse direction than the one you fear. He says it as a critique: the ideology of AGI, the belief that a particular endpoint is the inevitable destination, is itself the thing driving the industry toward bad outcomes.

Not an inevitability. Not a discovery. A choice. That claim, from a sitting Nobel laureate, ought to reset the public conversation about AI.

The most underreported move Autor makes is citing Max Kasy’s *The Means of Prediction.* Kasy is an economist at Oxford, and his metaphor is a medieval one. In the enclosure movement, the Lords took the commons — land that had been shared, worked, governed locally for generations — and declared it private. They were more efficient, perhaps, at farming. The commoners were wiped out.

The Internet, Kasy argues, is being enclosed the same way. The photos, the writing, the videos, the reviews, the code, all of it put out over decades under the assumption that it was common property, is being taken and monetized by a small number of firms. Your artwork now competes with you. Your stories are sold back to you. The property rights have been quietly transferred.

Autor names this. Acemoglu agrees. Stewart reframes it as reverse socialism. All three are right.

The move after that is Autor’s line, which Acemoglu immediately endorses: *AI is God’s gift to authoritarians.* The infrastructure being built for AI, centralized compute and centralized data and centralized training, is the same infrastructure required for mass surveillance. China is the proof of concept. The United States is privatizing its version.

And the fourth thing, brought in by Acemoglu, is Michael Sandel’s critique of meritocracy. The ideology that winners are virtuous and losers deserve their fate. Acemoglu says you cannot understand the rise of Trump without it.

They were not circling the small version of the problem. If you haven’t heard the podcast, go hear it.

## Before labor and capital

Most AI policy arguments still begin with the same two characters: labor and capital.

Almost every serious economic fight in America eventually returns to the table between them: wages, profits, bargaining power, ownership, taxation, redistribution. Stewart’s conversation lived mostly at that table, and rightly so. If machines increase output while workers lose leverage, the first question is obvious: who gets the gain?

Labor is what people can do. Capital is what people own. Trust is what lets either one cross the distance between strangers.

But before labor and capital can bargain, something quieter has to work.

A worker has to be legible as a worker. A credential has to mean something. A reference has to carry weight. A job posting has to describe a real job. A contract has to be more than theater. A résumé, an interview, a license, a review, a handshake, a name on a door: each of these is a small signal that lets strangers act without knowing everything. The signal carries information because it costs something to produce honestly and more to fake convincingly.

That is trust in its practical form.

Not trust as sentiment. Not trust as moral warmth. Trust as the working ability to move before full verification is possible. You do not test every vial before the nurse injects you. You do not inspect your neighbor’s wiring before you sleep. You do not audit the payroll system before taking a job.

Civilization is not the absence of fraud. Fraud is ancient. Civilization is the ability to move through the day without turning every ordinary exchange into a forensic investigation.

AI changes that price.

It makes plausible signals cheap. A tailored résumé, a polished cover letter, a fake review, a synthetic voice, a professional-looking memo, a citation-shaped hallucination, a video that appears to show someone saying something they never said. The issue is not that every signal becomes false. The issue is that the cost of making something look credible falls faster than the cost of checking whether it is credible.

You can see it already.

In hiring, [Greenhouse’s 2025 AI hiring report](https://www.greenhouse.com/newsroom/an-ai-trust-crisis-70-of-hiring-managers-trust-ai-to-make-faster-and-better-hiring-decisions-only-8-of-job-seekers-call-it-fair) found that 91 percent of recruiters had spotted candidate deception, and 34 percent now spend up to half their week filtering spam applications. Only 8 percent of job seekers called AI in hiring fair. The application used to be a bridge between employer and worker. Both sides now suspect the bridge is being gamed.

In commerce, [the Federal Trade Commission finalized a rule](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) banning fake reviews and AI-generated testimonials. That sounds like consumer protection, and it is. It is also an admission that one of the Internet’s basic trust mechanisms has been polluted enough to require federal force. The five-star review was a tiny civic instrument. Now it needs a cop.

In government, [Pew’s 2025 data](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/) put trust in Washington to do what is right just about always or most of the time at 17 percent. AI did not cause that collapse. But AI arrives inside it. It does not enter a healthy trust environment. It enters softened ground.

That is where the labor-capital frame starts to run out of road.

Wage insurance can soften the loss from a lower-paying job. Universal basic capital can give workers a claim on the upside. Tax reform can rebalance the incentives between hiring people and replacing them. But all three assume the same hidden substrate: that employers can identify real workers, workers can identify real opportunities, citizens can identify real institutions, and the public can still tell extraction from repair.

When that substrate weakens, the economy does not merely become unequal. It becomes unverifiable.

## The enclosure they didn’t name

Kasy’s enclosure framing lands right at this point. What the Lords took from the medieval commons was the land. What the tech firms are taking from the Internet is the data. But the deeper enclosure, the one that makes the policy menu fail if it goes unaddressed, is happening to the substrate underneath: to the capacity to verify at all.

The original commons were not just land. They were a village’s capacity to cooperate without contracts. To raise a barn without a foreman. To settle a dispute without a judge. That capacity was built by presence, by the cost of the honest signal, by the accumulated repetition of showing up.

The shape of the commons repeats across geographies. In the valleys where I live, it takes the form of the acequia (phonetic spelling: asequee-uh).

An acequia is not just a ditch. It is a social system with water in it. It has turns, memory, obligation, repair, argument, and a mayordomo who knows that the map is not what keeps the water moving. The people do.

Once a year, every spring, the neighbors walk the length of the ditch with shovels and clear it. The work is called la limpia. No foreman supervises. No contract compels. The ditch runs because showing up to clean it is the price of using it. What gets produced is not only water. It is the annual recognition that the neighbor is real, is owed, is here for another year.

The substrate and the governance are the same thing.

You could digitize the flow. You could optimize the schedule. You could build a model that predicts demand by parcel, crop, and season. Some of that might help. But if the underlying trust collapses, if people stop believing the turns are fair, if no one shows up to clear the silt, if upstream users quietly take more than their share, the dashboard becomes a beautiful picture of a dying system.

The Internet-era commons rested on this kind of substrate in digital form. You could trust a review because enough people had stakes in the platform not being a scam. You could trust a seller because the seller had skin in the game. You could trust a source because the institution behind it had a reputation to protect. The digital version was thinner, but it held. Until it didn’t.

What is being enclosed now is the substrate itself.

The attention required to verify is being drowned. Infinite AI-generated content competes for the same finite human attention budget. Nobody has time to check anymore, because nobody has time to read.

The intermediaries that used to catch the fraud are being dismantled on the same timeline as the load on them is increasing. Platform trust-and-safety teams cut. Call centers automated away. Local journalism dwindling. The scaffolding is removed the same quarter the pressure on it is raised.

The geographic proximity that enabled presence-based trust is being displaced by remote work, platform-mediated interaction, and algorithmic sorting that optimizes for engagement rather than the repeated cooperative encounter.

The substrate is being depleted faster than it regenerates.

The economists gestured at this when they said AI is God’s gift to authoritarians. They gestured at it when they said the Internet is being enclosed. They gestured at it when they cited Sandel on meritocracy. But the frame they were working in did not let them say what the third axis was. So they reached for policies on the two axes they had.

## What this means for the policy menu

Let me be fair to these policies before I say what they miss.

Wage insurance is Autor’s proposal. If you lose a job at twenty-five dollars an hour and can find one at fifteen, the federal government subsidizes part of the difference for up to two years. According to Autor, the Obama-era pilot paid for itself. It is politically viable because it rewards work. I support it. You should too.

Universal basic capital is also Autor’s. Every American gets an endowment at birth, invested in an index of productive capital, with voting rights attached. An ownership stake rather than a monthly check. Norway does something like this with its sovereign wealth fund from oil. It works.

Capital-versus-labor tax rebalance is Acemoglu’s. The United States has subsidized capital and taxed labor heavily since roughly 1980, and this biases firms toward automation at every margin. Fix the tax code; fix the margin.

Three policies. All real. Each one carries a hidden assumption.

Wage insurance assumes an employer will hire the displaced worker at a lower wage. If the employer no longer trusts that any given applicant is who they say they are, because AI has generated a hundred indistinguishable résumés that cannot be verified in the time available, the policy fails at the hiring moment.

The ownership stake that Universal Basic Capital creates has to retain value. If the companies being held are themselves the ones extracting the last of the trust commons, the stake appreciates briefly and collapses when the substrate does.

The same substrate problem applies to tax reform. If the tax code penalizes labor and subsidizes capital, as it has since 1980, the corrective is to rebalance. But rebalancing requires a working feedback loop between citizens and the state. If trust in government is depleted faster than any tax mechanism can regenerate it, the loop breaks. A state cannot collect revenue from citizens who do not believe the state will deliver. What fills the vacuum is not democracy. It is surveillance and force. The authoritarian floor Autor named at the start.

None of this makes the policies wrong in their own frame. It means the frame is too narrow.

The first task of AI policy is not only to protect wages or broaden ownership. It is to protect signal-cost: the price that makes honesty distinguishable from performance.

That changes what has to be on the menu.

Friction is not always inefficiency. Sometimes friction is the price of trust.

Consider three additions of the same weight.

*Signal custody at the point of production.* The default today is that every signal you generate, your writing, your voice, your health data, your location, your photos, is captured by the platform nearest its production and resold without you in the loop. The inverse is signal custody at the point of production. The producer retains technical control. Any onward use carries attached provenance: who produced it, under what conditions, with what consent. The protocols for this already exist in prototype form. Policy has not caught up to the protocols.

*Presence-weighted institutions.* Schools, clinics, courthouses, union halls, congregations, town councils, acequia associations: these produce trust substrate as a byproduct of operating. What they actually do, at the level of daily practice, is treat people as if their dignity matters. The substrate is what accumulates when that practice is repeated often enough, long enough, by enough people in the same room. They are currently taxed and regulated identically to institutions that generate no substrate at all. A presence-weighted policy would give preferential tax treatment, procurement preference, or zoning protection to institutions that generate the substrate, in recognition that they are providing a public good no other mechanism can produce. New Mexico water law already does this for one commons. The model is legible. It has not been generalized.

*Signal-cost floors.* Some classes of signal, a review, a credential, a political communication, a job posting, could be required by law to carry a minimum production cost. Verified identity. Mandatory latency. A witness layer. Staked commitment that can be slashed. We already do this in narrow sectors. Securities law requires statements that cost something to produce and carry liability if false. Extending the logic to the broader signal economy is not a new invention. It is an old regulatory principle applied to the substrate it now depends on.

These sound small. They are not small. They are the condition under which the other policies work.



------------------------------------------------------------------------



The ideology of AGI wants to skip that. It says: do not trust the nurse, the teacher, the electrician, the journalist, the neighbor, the worker, the local institution, the craft, the slow accumulation of judgment. Trust the model. Trust the platform. Trust the algorithm. Trust the company that owns the system and calls its incentives alignment.

That is not only an economic substitution. It is a civic substitution.

A model can summarize, detect, predict, accelerate, and assist. But a model has not cleaned the ditch. It has not sat with the patient. It has not watched a student struggle honestly toward a sentence. It has not stood behind its work with a name, a place, a memory, and a reputation that can be damaged.

The human signal is not valuable because humans are magical. It is valuable because humans can be accountable in ways machines cannot. They can be embarrassed. They can be exiled. They can be forgiven. They can show up next season.

That is what the labor-capital frame has trouble seeing. It is built for allocation. It can price a job, tax a profit, insure a wage, distribute a share. But trust is not distributed like a dividend. It is grown, maintained, depleted, and sometimes lost.



------------------------------------------------------------------------



Stewart’s conversation mattered because it got so close. The guests saw the authoritarian risk. They saw the enclosure. They saw the cruelty hiding inside meritocracy. But under all three sat something they did not name.

What I kept hearing was trust. Not as sentiment. As a physical capacity built from presence, from the cost of honest signals, from the repetition of showing up. And beneath that: the capacity to still recognize each other as real, and to act as if that recognition matters.

In the desert, you do not repair a water system by trading futures on the ditch.

You clean the ditch. You settle the turns. You make the flow visible enough that the next person can believe it will still be there.

Earth Day was a few days ago. The ditch will be maintained or it will fragment.

I have my shovel. Will you meet me at the ditch with yours?
