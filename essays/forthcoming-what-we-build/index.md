What We Build
On Constraint, Presence, and the Floor We Have to Build

> *Policy is a sign posted above a river. Show me your physics.*

Two essays of diagnosis. The pipeline that moves a signal through you before you can vote on it. The softened ground that lets a cheap signal outrun a costly one, and the slow ruin no one chose. If you've stayed with me this far, you've earned the turn.

The answer is not a better wall. It's a different ground.

I want to be careful about what I mean, because the word *physics* gets thrown around as decoration and I'm not using it as decoration. I mean a constraint that holds the way gravity holds — not a rule you comply with, not a policy you can lobby away, but a condition the system is built inside. You don't audit your gravitational posture quarterly. You don't get an exemption. Gravity is just the floor under everything else. The question this essay asks is whether trust can be given a floor like that. I think it can — but only if we're honest about the parts that don't exist yet.

## The law, stated plainly

The constraint has a name and a shorthand. Autonomy cannot exceed environment. `A ≤ E`.

The freedom any agent can exercise is bounded by the environment's capacity to absorb the consequences of that freedom and recover from them. A model cannot be safely more autonomous than the system watching it can understand. A platform cannot extract more than the ecosystem feeding it can survive. A signal cannot move faster than the environment can verify it without the environment degrading. This is the same law I've been writing under since Part 1, when I told you that repair was losing the race. *Repair losing the race* is `A ≤ E` violated: autonomy of signal outrunning capacity of environment.

I have to be straight about my stake in this, because the rest of the essay isn't honest without it. You don't invent `A ≤ E` any more than you invent gravity — you notice it, usually in the wreckage of the systems that ignored it. I've spent the better part of two decades inside systems that failed, and the last few of them building one thing that tries to honor the constraint instead of pretending it away: a protocol called the Kinetic Trust Protocol. It's mine, and I should say that plainly, because what follows is a bet I'm making with my own work on the table — not a finished product I'm selling you a shine on. The constraint is older than me. The attempt to build to it is mine, unfinished, and I'll show you the holes. Living inside this material for years has cost me something, too; I'll get to that in the last essay, because it turns out the cost has a name.

The move that matters is what it does to the question you ask. Today, every defense asks a question that's easy to manipulate and getting easier: *should we trust this?* Trust this account, this image, this claim, this source. It's the wrong question, because a sophisticated adversary can forge the answer all day, and because the emergent dynamics of the last decade have been corroding our collective ability to answer it at all. `A ≤ E` asks a different question: *what are the verifiable constraints that make trust possible here?* Not is this true — what would the environment have to enforce for falsity to be expensive?

A garden makes the point better than an abstraction. A rose left to sprawl runs along the ground and roots where it falls and gives you a thicket. A rose put against a trellis climbs — higher, stronger, into more sun — *because* something bounds it. The constraint is not the enemy of the bloom. The constraint is the condition of the bloom. `A ≤ E` is the trellis. We have spent a decade pulling out the trellises and calling the sprawl freedom, and wondering why nothing flowers.

## Four objections, before anything else

A trust architecture is a dangerous thing to propose, because the obvious version of it is a surveillance state with better branding. So before I tell you what a floor would change, I have to walk through the four ways it goes wrong. These are the four that keep me up, because I haven't fully answered any of them.

**Anonymity is not the enemy.** Any system that requires everyone to be identifiable is a surveillance architecture no matter what it's for. Whistleblowers, dissidents, abuse survivors, people exploring an identity they can't yet say out loud — all of them depend on speaking without revealing who they are. A "verified web" tied to real names and government IDs would be the perfect machine for a police state, even if it was built to stop bots. So the honest version of this does not ask *who are you?* It asks *are you real?* — and the distance between those two questions is the whole design.

In the physical world, trust is throttled by your body. You can't be in a thousand rooms at once or whisper in a million ears; your influence is bounded by the scarcity of your time and your presence. The pathology of the feed is not that people are anonymous. It's that speaking, amplifying, and coordinating cost nothing — that one operator can manufacture a million accounts and a fake consensus without a single one of them ever being tethered to a person who exists somewhere. So what a presence-based floor makes expensive is not anonymity. It's *placelessness*. It asks for proof of presence, not proof of identity: evidence that a signal came from a real human who spent the scarcity of their time and body to make it, without that human ever revealing their name. The cryptographers have a tool that gets partway there — a zero-knowledge proof, which lets you verify that something is true without revealing the data behind it. You prove the quality of your signal without surrendering the content of your life. Anonymity with accountability.

Make that concrete. A teacher posts an anonymous warning about a school-board decision — no name, no face, nothing that ties the words to the person who could be fired for them. Under a presence-based floor — if the hard part works, which I'll be honest about in a moment — that warning still carries weight, because one real human spent real presence to send it, and the system can confirm that without ever learning who she is. Now a single operator spins up ten thousand accounts to bury her. They share no presence between them — no bodies, no places, no time spent in the world — so the floor reads them as the one signal they are, not the ten thousand they claim to be. The teacher stays anonymous and stays heard. The operator stays anonymous and stays *one*. Anonymity preserved, multiplication denied — which is exactly what identity-based systems get backwards: they unmask the teacher and let the operator hide in the crowd.

But I have to be honest about how much weight one word — *presence* — is carrying, because it's the hinge of the whole thing, and it's the place I'm least able to tell you it's solved. A zero-knowledge proof can attest that some credential is valid without exposing it. What it cannot do, by itself, is prove that a living body moved through the world. That takes a chain of attestations about presence — a device, a place, a span of time — that someone still has to issue, and that someone could still forge or sell. Making *presence* provable, and not merely identity, is the open engineering problem at the center of this. What I can defend is the shape of the goal: move the cost of faking a human from zero to something a bot farm can't pay. A bot farm can still generate infinite text; it simply carries no weight, because it can prove no presence. To fake a consensus, an attacker would have to spend scarce human presence at real scale. The lie doesn't become impossible. It becomes expensive. Expensive is the whole game.

**The economics are real and brutal.** Building this means disrupting the business model of the most valuable companies on earth. The engagement economy that softens the ground monetizes attention at a scale that crossed a trillion dollars in 2024, and the platforms that profit from frictionless amplification will not volunteer for anything that makes manipulation expensive, because the same friction that stops manipulation also raises the cost of the engagement they sell. This is not a technical problem and I won't pretend it is. Physics describes the constraint. Politics decides whether the constraint gets built. Every structural reform in history — food safety, emissions, financial regulation — is the story of the interests that profit from the status quo fighting exactly this. Anything real here will face that fight. The only question is whether the cost of *not* building it becomes undeniable before the window closes.

**Who verifies the verifiers?** If the answer is government, you've built an instrument of state speech control. If it's the platforms, you've handed the arsonists the fire code. If it's one central technical body, you've made a single point of capture. The only answer that doesn't reproduce the disease is that whatever gets built must be open, auditable, and owned by no one — its constraints inspectable by anyone the way the rules of arithmetic are inspectable by anyone. No institution gets to be the sole arbiter of what counts as real. This is the hardest engineering problem in the whole framework and I will not tell you it's solved. But the alternative — trust governed by opaque algorithms owned by private companies accountable to no one — is not a neutral baseline. It's a system that already failed. The question is not whether to build something better. It's whether the better thing concentrates power or distributes it, and it has to distribute it, or it becomes the thing it was built to prevent.

**And who gets shut out by the floor?** This is the one that should worry you most, because a system built to make manipulation expensive can make *legitimate* speech expensive for the people who can least afford it. Proof of presence is cheap if you have a stable device, a fixed address, a documented life, and a country where being seen is safe. It is not cheap if you are undocumented, unhoused, rural, poor, disabled, a child, someone inside a coercive household, or someone in a place where proving you were *here* is the thing that gets you killed. A floor that quietly requires those proofs doesn't only stop bot farms — it disenfranchises the vulnerable and calls it safety, and then it isn't a floor at all. It's another wall, pointed the usual direction. I don't have this solved either, and I won't pretend the gesture at anonymity above covers it. What I can say is that it's the design constraint, not a footnote to it: a presence proof that can't be earned by the person with the least has failed, however well it stops the person with the most. If the floor excludes the weak to bind the strong, throw it out — it became the thing it was built to prevent.

## What it changes

With those four nailed down, look at what a floor under trust does to the ground we've been standing on.

It changes the payoff matrix without needing a villain. The emergent erosion of Part 2 happens because nothing in the environment requires that attention be earned rather than extracted. A presence-based floor supplies that requirement structurally. When autonomy is bounded by verifiability, a system that extracts attention through manipulation meets resistance built into the physics — not because a regulator showed up, but because the unverifiable signal now costs more and carries less. You don't have to win the prisoner's dilemma. You change the board it's played on.

Go back to Sydney. An innocent man, Benjamin Cohen, was named as the mass-murderer — and the name went out first from a place a presence floor can't touch: a television network, fully credentialed, fully present, broadcasting a mistake. The floor would not have stopped that. What it would have stopped is what came next. The error became a fourteen-hour manufactured reality not because one broadcaster erred but because a placeless cascade — accounts with no body, no place, no time spent in the world — multiplied the single mistake into tens of thousands of *confirmations*. Under a presence-based floor those accounts still exist and stay anonymous, but they carry no weight; the platform reads their absence of presence and treats the swarm as the few real signals it actually is. The broadcaster's error stays a broadcaster's error — correctable, attributable, one. The manufactured consensus that made it unkillable never forms. A nation-state can still pay to fake presence at scale; a bored operator with a hundred thousand sock puppets cannot. *Slow and bounded* is a different war than *free and infinite*, and it's a war repair can actually run in.

And it offers the trapped defender a third move. Part 2 left the defender with two bad options: act aggressively and erode the trust you depend on, or act gently and stay ineffective. The floor is a third option, because it doesn't ask anyone to judge the *message* — the trap that defeats every content-moderation regime. It asks whether the conditions under which a message propagates — presence, coordination, amplification — are verifiable. You don't have to decide whether a narrative is true if you can determine whether the consensus around it is organic or manufactured. You don't have to judge the messenger if you can verify that the messenger is real. Cognitive warfare succeeds wherever trust is *assumed* rather than verified — deepfakes work because we trust our eyes, fake movements work because we trust that a crowd is a crowd. A floor doesn't end those attacks. It makes trust testable instead of assumable, and that is the ground every other defense — detection, literacy, deterrence — needs in order to function at all.

## Why now

Why is this conversation happening in 2026 and not 2016? The engagement economy, the emergent erosion, the exploitation by adversaries — none of it is new. Researchers have been describing it for a decade.

What changed is the terrain. In late 2022, general-purpose AI became a consumer product, and within months the cost of generating persuasive text, image, and video collapsed to near zero. The asymmetry between offense and defense, already bad, went exponential — an operation that once needed a team and a season can now be prototyped in an afternoon. That's the threat half, and it's why the window is closing.

But the same shift opened the other half. The tools that can manufacture manipulation at scale can also model trust systems, map information flows, test protocol constraints, and simulate adversaries at a depth the people who built the old institutional frameworks never had. The structural answer isn't an old idea anyone overlooked. It's an idea that became *buildable* the moment the terrain shifted — and the people who understand the shift have a narrow lead over the forces, emergent and deliberate, already exploiting it.

And the stakes are not abstract, because trust is not an opinion. It's the substrate every critical system runs on. When institutional trust erodes far enough, it doesn't show up first in a poll. It shows up in whether the grid stays on, whether the water from the tap is safe, whether the trucks reach the shelves — because every one of those systems runs on coordinated human trust, and when that trust degrades, the systems don't fail all at once. They get slower, more brittle, more vulnerable to the next shock. And the next shock is always coming. The election cycles I keep naming — 2028, 2030, 2032, 2036 — are just the visible clock. The real clock is the childhood I described in Part 2, and it's running faster.

## Show me your physics

So that's the build. Not a wall — a floor. Not *should we trust this*, but *what makes trust verifiable here*. Not the policing of speech, but a constraint on placelessness that leaves anonymity intact and makes manufactured consensus expensive. A trellis, so the thing can finally climb.

I've spent two decades inside systems that failed, and I've learned to ask one question when someone tells me their system is safe. Not *what's your policy*. Policy is a sign posted above a river. *Show me your physics.* Show me the constraint that holds when no one's watching, the floor that's there on a Tuesday when the regulator's asleep and the incentive says cheat. If there's no physics, there's no safety — there's only a promise, and we have learned what promises are worth in this medium.

But a floor is not a home. You can build the most honest ground in the world and still have to decide what to grow on it — and that decision isn't physics. It's older than physics, and it's tended by hand, and it lives about a mile from the house I grew up in. That's where we're going last.

Somewhere a child is still saying "six seven," and the channel that carried it hasn't closed and won't. The only question this whole series has been circling is what we choose to send through it — and whether the ground beneath it can hold the weight of an answer. Now we have the shape of a ground that could. What we plant in it is the last essay, and the most important one.
