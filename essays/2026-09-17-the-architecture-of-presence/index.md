The Architecture of Presence
On the one thing this newsletter has been building.

A confession first.

For the past few months, every issue of this newsletter has been about the same thing. I didn't tell you and I'm not sure if you noticed.

- The vault and the ditch.
- The human in the loop who was only ever there to take the blame.
- The four clocks.
- The expiring lease.

Each piece arrived as its own argument. Each one turned out to be a room in a building I hadn't named.

Here is the name. The architecture of presence.

And here is the idea underneath it, as plainly as I can say it.

    Civilization is currently deciding — mostly by accident — where human judgment still lives.

    Every deployment of an autonomous system is a small relocation.

    Some judgment that used to sit with a person now rides in a pipeline, a policy, a model, a default.

    Almost none of these relocations are decided as relocations.

    They are decided as efficiencies.

    One at a time.

    Each one reasonable.

    And the sum is a map nobody drew.

    Emergence.

The work is deciding it on purpose. Because the question is not whether judgment moves. It already has. The question is what must remain possible after it moves.

### Why now, and not gradually

One asymmetry makes this urgent instead of academic.

Capability scales. Accountable human judgment doesn't scale in the same way.

- The carried layer: The agents, the pipelines, the engines that execute judgment settled somewhere else — just multiplied by orders of magnitude. It got faster than every accountable clock we have.
- The held layer: The people who can actually be argued with, who change their minds while it still matters — is the same size it always was. Individual humans. We need sleep. We wear out.

When the fast layer meets the slow one, I keep finding the same conversion in the record. A living judgment gets recognized by a larger institution. Recognition requires making it legible. The veto becomes a procedure. The judgment becomes a filing. The relationship becomes a record. Something is preserved. Something else moves.

I've watched it happen too many times to call it coincidence. Machine speed doesn't change the pattern, it changes whether the conversion can ever be undone.

> Once the record becomes executable, the representation of judgment can begin acting faster than the people whose judgment it represents.

The machine doesn't merely preserve the representation anymore. The representation can act.

Read that again: the representation can act.

### The three moves

Every piece of this architecture makes some combination of three moves.

One: locate the judgment. Before you can protect a thing, you have to know where it is.

Who holds the judgment here, and who merely carries it? Is the thing you govern a record, which must not change — or a rhythm, which must? ([I drew that line in an earlier issue.](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/historical-society-kinetic-chris-perkins-ozz4c))

Which parts of a decision belong to physics, which to law, which to a lease — and which judgment cannot be surrendered merely because the machinery can execute it?

[Robin Martherus](https://www.linkedin.com/in/martherus) gave me part of the language for this in [Tamed Autonomy](https://TamedAutonomy.ai). CAN and SHOULD are different gates. An agent can be perfectly capable of an action, properly authorized to attempt it, and still arrive at a moment where the action should not continue.

> No amount of CAN, by itself, produces SHOULD.

I think presence is part of what keeps the second gate alive. It'd ought to.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two: keep it alterable until consequence. Presence is not attention. A camera pays attention. A thermostat responds. A learning system updates. Presence is narrower, and harder. To be present is to remain alterable by the other — by evidence, by the person the decision lands on, while the decision still runs.

Most of what we call oversight fails this test.

- A reviewer anchored to a finished recommendation.
- A signature on an outcome already fixed.
- [A person in the room only to absorb the blame](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/human-loop-take-blame-chris-perkins-lze4c) after the consequential choice has already hardened.

There's a way to make part of this checkable — call it proof of attention, though what it proves is not that someone's mind was on the problem. Internal states leave no reliable audit field.

What it proves is that the review happened while the outcome could still move: what evidence entered, what response it produced, what authority the reviewer held, and whether the decision could still change. A neighbor can verify that. A rubber stamp can't.

The machinery worth building — the expiring lease, the mandatory hand-back, the gate where the people re-enter — exists for one purpose and one purpose only. When something is about to change a life, there is still someone who can be argued with. And still time for the argument to matter.

There's a name for the move, and I've spent a book on it. Break the Chain.

In security, the kill chain is the attacker's sequence, and the art is severing it before it completes — at machine speed, because that's the speed it runs at. Machine judgment has a chain too: sense, score, decide, act, act again, faster than anyone watching.

Find the link where consequence turns irreversible, and break the chain there — not to stop the machine, but to route the decision to a body with standing to say yes, no, or not yet. A council. A board. An adjudicator. An analyst. A consortium of the affected. A community. [Us](https://chrisperkins505.medium.com/she-had-three-rules-dcdb624f52cc?source=friends_link&sk=d2d9e9c3deb41789cde2c1ce6c53dd9e).

> Breaking the chain isn't the failure of automation. It's the design that lets automation be trusted with the rest.

The right link is the last one where judgment can still change the consequential trajectory. Automate freely before it, where the turn is settled. Return authority there, when it isn't.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three: make repair structural. A system that can act again after failure needs more than a record of what failed. Something has to change before authority is restored. The damage repaired. The conditions reset. The next grant of authority has to know something the last one didn't. Otherwise you have feedback without consequence.

The system records the failure and repeats the arrangement. Sound familiar?

The commons that governed themselves for centuries knew this. The acequia is cleaned every spring, by everyone who draws from it, before the water runs.

> A system without structural repair isn't a loop. It's a cascade with good posture.

### What the commons already knew

The person who mapped this was Elinor Ostrom. She won the Nobel in economics in 2009 — the first woman to — for showing that communities govern shared resources across generations without requiring either centralized management or privitization. ([Trust, in these systems, is an integral — it accumulates over time.](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-trust-integral-3209442798ad))

She found eight design principles the durable ones shared. Read them as a checklist for any system that has to be governed. (I've put a kitchen-table version next to each, because you already live inside them.)

1. CLEAR BOUNDARIES

- The neighborhood pool has a gate and a key, and only the families who pay dues get one. Everyone knows who belongs and what belonging costs. A commons with no edge gets drained by people who never chipped in.

2. RULES FIT PLACE

- The rules of a desert water district and a rainy one cannot be the same rules. In Las Cruces you ration year-round; somewhere wetter you'd never write that clause. Good rules are cut to the ground they govern, not copied from a binder built somewhere else.

3. THE GOVERNED HELP WRITE THE RULES

- The people who actually swim in the pool have a hand in setting the pool hours — not a distant office that never gets in the water. If a rule is going to land on you, you get a say in shaping it.

4. MONITORING THAT ANSWERS TO THE GOVERNED

- On a shared dirt road, whoever watches the potholes answers to the neighbors who use it, or is one of them — not to a boss on a hill. The monitor is accountable to the people, not above them.

5. GRADUATED SANCTIONS

- The first time your kid's soccer club misses a field cleanup, someone has a quiet word. The tenth time, you're off the schedule. Punishment starts small and climbs — so one bad week doesn't cost you everything, and a pattern still does.

6. ACCESSIBLE CONFLICT RESOLUTION

- Two neighbors argue over a fence line and settle it cheaply and close to home, not in a courtroom two years and ten thousand dollars later. If settling a dispute costs more than the dispute, the commons quietly dies of legal bills.

7. RIGHT TO ORGANIZE

- The city lets the community garden run its own plots and its own waitlist instead of dissolving it into Parks & Rec. The larger authority recognizes the small one's right to govern itself.

8. NESTED GOVERNANCE

- Your street handles its own block party; the neighborhood association handles the shared park; the city handles the water main. Small units sit inside larger ones, each governing what it can see, coordinated across as well as up and down the layers.

Read together they do the one thing I care about. They keep governance close enough to the governed that information, conflict, sanction, and revision can still move through the system.

The judgment stays where the consequence lands.

And my trust-network model predicts a candidate ninth principle: renewal of the relationships that hold a commons together. I derive that requirement within the model in Ostrom's Design Principles as Percolation Maintenance Specifications for Trust Networks: A Ninth Principle Predicted by Network Dynamics. Repair before renewed authority is the institutional requirement I draw from it. ([https://doi.org/](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147)[10.5281](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147)[/](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147)[zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147)[.](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147)[21209147](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21209147))

The model's warning: a commons can look intact while the relationships holding it together decay.

> A tragedy of the commons is a lack of creativity.

### The rooms of the building

If you've been reading Digital Gravity, you've already been inside.

The Historical Society and the Kinetic Society located the judgment — rest and motion, the record and the turn.

The Human in the Loop piece found the counterfeit — answerability installed where control never was.

The Short Lease, which I promised you and which arrives next, is the alterability machinery in full: authority that expires on its own and returns the decision before the conditions that justified it go stale.

There's a room outside this newsletter, too.

The Open Trust Commons is a registry I helped start that does something most registries refuse to do — it declines to grade its own homework. It won't certify that a system is trustworthy. It won't give you five stars or any reviews.

It records what projects claim, what evidence supports those claims, and what remains unverified. It leaves the judging to the people who use it. On the human clock.

A commons that governs the record without pretending to own the verdict. That is one room of the architecture, being built, in public, in code: <https://github.com/open-trust-commons/otcs-registry>

> The rooms connect. That's the claim the name makes.

This was never a collection of takes about AI governance. It's one building. And the reason it needs building is simple to say:

We are keeping a place for the person in systems that increasingly do not need one in order to proceed.

Not because every turn requires a person. Because some turns become illegitimate when no person can still alter them.

### The reader's stake

You don't work on protocols, maybe. But you do stand at gates.

Somewhere in your week there is a decision that arrives already assembled. Verified. Scored. Framed. Finished except for the click. Yes or no.

The architecture of presence is the discipline of asking one question in the moment... and in motion.

Can I still be changed here? Can the person this lands on still change what happens next? Beautiful questions. Practice asking them.

> If the answer is no, you are not overseeing the system. You are its costume.

The institution under study now is the one where you sit.

So what must remain possible, after the judgment moves, has a name.

- Not butts in seats.
- Not a human in the loop or in the crumple zone.
- Not signatures, not attention, not an approval button glowing at the end of a finished pipeline.

What must remain is... the continued possibility that evidence, consequence, or another person can still change the judgment before the judgment changes the world.

That is presence. That is the architecture. The work is keeping a place for it.

I don't stand for pacing or stopping the machines for its own sake. I don't stand for putting a human in every loop out of nostalgia. And I don't stand for calling it oversight when the answer was settled before anyone with standing could touch it.

I stand for questions.

And for building the kind of systems in which the answer can still matter — even for the people who can't be in the room to give it.

More rooms soon. The building is going up.
