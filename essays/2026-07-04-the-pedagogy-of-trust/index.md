The Pedagogy of Trust
On the death of the job, not work — and the pledge it taught for free

*Our Moon. July 4th, 2023. Photo by me. Las Cruces, New Mexico.*

Every spring, up and down the Rio Grande, the old acequia communities of New Mexico hold *la limpia.* The people who share a ditch walk its length with shovels and clear out a winter of silt and branch and leaf. Nobody is paid. The water will not move unless everyone shows up, so everyone shows up. By noon you know who is reliable and who is not, who came late and who stayed.

You have re-learned, with your hands, who your neighbors are.

That is work. The old kind — ten thousand years old. Not the wage. The showing-up that tells a community who it can count on.

This year the country turns two hundred and fifty and will say independence until the word shines thin. But the Declaration did not end in aloneness. It ended in pledge: lives, fortunes, sacred honor, offered mutually, one signer to another. The act we remember as independence was also an act of dependence rightly named.

That is the part we have forgotten.

I want to tell you work is dying now. That is not quite right, and the "not quite" is the whole of what follows. So let me write the obituary carefully, because we are, most of us, standing at the wrong funeral.

---

## The notice

WORK, of Earth, is being declared dead in the country's two-hundred-fiftieth year — of automation, cause widely given as the machines.

It is the wrong notice.

Work is not dead; you watched it on the ditch-bank this spring. What died is younger and narrower and easy to mistake for the whole: *the job.* The wage, the title, the career with a ladder, the weekend, retirement — the whole arrangement by which a person *has a job* that *is their place in the world.* That bundle is barely two centuries old. Older arrangements paid people for labor; this one made the job a person's place in the world, and it is younger than the steam engine. We built it after the last time a machine came for a way of living, and because none of us were alive before it, we mistook the settlement for the weather.

So: not the death of work. The death of the job. Which is a strange thing to say in an anniversary year dedicated to independence, because the job was one of the places modern Americans learned dependence rightly ordered: how to be needed without being owned, how to be counted on without being consumed. Hold onto the distinction, because the comfortable will spend the next decade getting the name wrong — some by accident, and some by design.

And the name matters because the job did more than pay us. It assigned us to one another. It gave us a place to be expected, a name by which to be missed, a craft by which to become useful, and a public rhythm by which trust repaired itself without ever calling itself trust.

Work made us necessary. The job made us legible.

Automation can take many tasks. It cannot, by itself, replace that hidden curriculum — and that is the crisis underneath the crisis.

> The job was an accidental school for trust. As it dies, trust has to become intentional.

A country founded on a mutual pledge has to learn, again, how pledge becomes practice.

There is a single object at the center of this death, and a woman saw all the way through it in 1843. We will come to her. First, the life.

---

## The life

An obituary owes the dead an honest account, and the job's begins in a river.

I have written before that [the Internet is a straightened river](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-pedagogy-of-digital-physics-20c6cb74782c). I grew up on the Rio Grande, watching engineers do to the water what we are now doing to a mind: take a living system full of meanders and side channels and slack water — all the so-called inefficiency — and straighten it into a channel that moves more, faster, and holds nothing alive. [The silvery minnow died when we straightened the river.](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-straightening-3b662dbf389e) Run that same operation on an economy and you get the job.

Strip the metaphor and the mechanism is dull and exact. You take a system that spent centuries optimizing for resilience and you re-optimize it for throughput. The resilience lived in the redundancy — the meanders, the side channels, the slack water no engineer could ever justify on a spreadsheet. Throughput reads redundancy as waste and removes it. The system runs faster and fails in a new way, because the slack you deleted was load-bearing. That is the pattern in a river and in an economy both: optimize one variable hard enough and you consume the substrate that made the variable possible.

Before the factory, people lived inside what the historian E.P. Thompson called the moral economy: the just price, customary right, the commons you could glean and graze, the obligation that ran between a master and the apprentice at his elbow. Strip the romance and it was a trust network — exchange priced by relationship, not by the hour. It was a protocol, not a sentiment. It specified who owed what to whom, and it was enforced by proximity: by the fact that you would meet the other party again, in the same small place, for the rest of your life. That was the meander. That was the slack water where things lived.

Industrialization straightened it. It priced labor by the hour and called the obligation friction. It decoupled price from relationship — and once labor had an hourly rate, the obligation became a line item, and line items get optimized away. It also manufactured the scarcity that made the wage the only door. In 1845 the economist Frédéric Bastiat joked that the candlemakers ought to petition the government to block out the sun, since free sunlight was unfair competition. The Enclosure Acts did, in effect, what Bastiat's candlemakers only imagined: across a long century they blocked one of the old suns, fencing common land — land that had let families live partly outside the wage — and making the wage harder to refuse.

The job was predeceased by everything that had to die first for it to be born. By the commons, enclosed. By the apprenticeship, broken into motions simple enough that no one held the whole and no child inherited a trade. By task-time — sun and season, the job done when the job was done — replaced by the whistle and the clock. We remember the people who saw it happen and tried to stop it as fools; we named a slur after them. But the Luddites were not breaking machines because they feared machines. They were skilled textile workers fighting a political economy — breaking the specific machines being run, in their own words, "in a fraudulent and deceitful manner," to dump the cost onto the worker. They were asking who controls the loom and who eats the loss. The question got them hanged and remembered as backward.

It was a long life, and a real one. But the part the optimists skip is in the will. The wage did not simply lag for a bad year; in the heart of what economists call the Engels pause, output rose while real wages scarcely moved, and a cohort paid the whole bill for a prosperity they did not live to collect. When someone tells you the future of work will be fine, they are right on the scale of a century and lying on the scale of a life.

Ask them the only question that matters. *Fine on whose clock?*

## The cause of death

A careful obituary names the cause precisely, and "the machines" is not precise. Here is the coroner's finding — and it is the reason this death is not the apocalypse it will be sold as. *We have seen it before.*

The river died this way when throughput outran the floodplain. The bank dies this way when leverage outruns trust. The commons dies this way when appetite outruns governance. And the town built on the one mine or the one mill died this way when the work left — the men who stayed began dying at sixty-four, of a despair the statistics finally had to give a name. One cause under all of them: something the system ran on was slow and costly — the floodplain, the trust, the governance, the work itself — and it was removed or outrun, until the taking ran to completion and the thing came apart. They did not coordinate. They died the same kind of death: autonomy outran the environment that held it.

The job is dying this way now: automation is outrunning the moral settlement that made wage labor bearable. It is the latest in a line. Industrialization was capital outrunning the moral economy that once held it; the labor movement spent a century re-meandering the channel by hand — the weekend and the family wage were dug back in, not handed down. Now the automation of cognition is the same water cutting the same straight line, except faster than any settlement can be built to slow it.

And a death is never evenly shared. This one is landing first and hardest on two. On the men the last straightening already broke — those without a degree, in the places the work left, who learned at sixty-four that a country can stop needing you and never say so. And on the young arriving now to find the bottom rung sawn off — the entry jobs, the first reads and the first drafts, the apprentice's corner of every trade, are the places the new loom reaches first.

The old hand with no trade left to teach, and the young hand with no door to come in by: the two ends of the apprenticeship, both standing in the cold.

## The loom

Now the object at the center of it: the loom.

The machine that broke the handloom weaver was the power loom: it displaced his labor and bled his wages over a generation while the mills' output soared. But the machine Ada Lovelace saw through was a different one — the Jacquard loom, governed by punched cards, the machine that made pattern programmable at industrial scale. Lovelace looked at it and understood it was not about cloth. "We may say most aptly," she wrote, "that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves." Her question was never *can the machine think.* It was *what is the loom allowed to weave* — what constrains it. The loom constrains the weaver. The environment constrains the agent. I built [The Physics of Trust](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-physics-of-trust-3416d9c9efea) on that one sentence.

So one machine was the economic wound and the other was the prophecy, and together they are a single object turned in the light. It killed the weaver. It deskilled the craft. And it is the oldest picture we have of the only question that matters now — who builds the loom, and what is it permitted to weave.

The handloom of 1820 and the model in your pocket are the same object, two centuries apart.

It also tells you exactly what died and what did not, because a machine has never once taken our work. It takes our repetitions. Every machine age pries the repeatable part of a job away from the irreplaceable part, pays a machine for the first, and hands you back the second. The first straightening ate repeatable muscle and left us the mind; we built the whole modern middle class out of the leftover. This one eats repeatable cognition — the memo, the first read, the boilerplate. The paralegal, the analyst, the junior copywriter, the first-pass reviewer — each is standing somewhere near the handloom weaver of 1825, in the spot the loom is walking toward.

Which means the job is survived by whatever could never be made repeatable.

Presence survives — a body in a room, in a situation that has never happened in quite that way before.
Accountability survives — someone who can be held to it, who can be sorry, who has something at stake.
Judgment survives — the call made under real risk that no model will sign its name to.
Repair survives — and we will come back to why it survives last of all.

It is survived, too, by its children, who do not yet know what to do with their hands: by Skill, with no one left to be good for; by Trade, who taught your grandfather and has no apprentice at the door; by Craft and Calling and Mastery, old names with nowhere to be on Monday.

Notice what the survivors are. They are the part of every job that was always the most human — the part we did for free while we were paid for the repetitions. That is the whole inheritance, and the whole fight: as the repeatable falls toward cheapness, the human part becomes the contested estate.

Two things, though, that the last straightening promised and this one will not. Last time there was higher ground — muscle gave way to mind, and the mind was a larger country than the body. This time the water is rising toward the high ground itself; there may be no more ladder up. So the way out is not up. It is sideways — into the one register that was never about repetition at all. And last time the flood took three generations, slow enough that a weaver could tell his son to learn the new machine. This one moves at the speed of software. The thing you retrain into can be gone before the training ends.

We do not get to be calm about the clock.

## An honest word

You are supposed to speak well of the dead. You are also supposed to tell the truth, and the truth about the job is that it never loved us and it never robbed us. Like the ditch, it required us — your body, somewhere, at a certain hour, whether you felt like it or not. That was the whole of it. And the same requirement that wore you down was the one that set the same faces beside you, morning after morning, until you knew them.

The ditch does not love you. It requires you. And the requiring is what makes the neighbors.

What it gave, that we did not know we were being given: the job re-knit you into the human fabric every morning without anyone naming it — the unchosen people you had to make peace with by Thursday, the being-counted-on by name. It was how a society repaired its own trust on a daily cycle, for free, as a side effect of needing you somewhere at a certain hour. Pull the job and you do not only remove the wage. You remove the repair.

> You can pay a floor overnight. You cannot pay belonging overnight; it accretes at the speed of presence.

And what it took: the job was also the thing that cut a person off — from the thing they made, from the hours of their own life, from the others on the line. I will not stand at the graveside and pretend otherwise. We are burying the thing that wore us down and made our neighbors in one motion. So the honest grief is selective — grieve the repair, and refuse to grieve the estrangement, and ask whether this time we might keep the one and finally bury the other.

## The services

In lieu of despair, here is what the living do.

In [The Straightening](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-straightening-3b662dbf389e) I wrote that every new bosque starts with someone deciding the straight channel wasn't the only option. A bosque is what grows back when you let a river meander again — the cottonwoods, the side channels, the slack water where the minnow lives. You do not legislate a bosque. You stop straightening, you put the meanders back by hand, and the living returns because the conditions for living returned.

The economy on the far side of the job is a bosque or it is nothing. And the work of teaching people to live in restored water needs a name, because its opposite already has one. The thing that trains a person to be frictionless and extractable — the boarding school, the feed, the whole apparatus that turns a human into throughput — is the pedagogy of extraction. Its counter, the one this sequence of essays has been circling without naming, is the **pedagogy of trust**.

It is the curriculum the job used to run for free and will not anymore: how to trust and be trusted, taught on purpose. And its first rule is the one the framework keeps arriving at — it is embodied or it is nothing. You do not argue a person into trust. You teach it the way the ditch teaches it: on the bank, once a year, with your body, beside your neighbor, because the water will not move unless everyone shows up.

Read its lessons against everything this death has taken, and you find they are those losses, run backwards — each one a thing some named death removed, put back by hand.

Where the field was enclosed, it teaches you to find ground no one can fence: the floor that lets you say no, the commons, the open sky.

Where presence was stripped out for efficiency, it teaches you to show up with your body somewhere you could have scrolled: the shared meal, the repair day, the crew, the clinic, the choir, the fire hall.

Where the future was cut and the trade went unhanded-down, it teaches the fit between hand, tool, task, and person: one older hand, one younger hand, one thing worth learning slowly.

Where the taking ran to completion, it puts the ceiling back: nothing extracts more than the ground can hold and still live.

Where the betrayal went unanswered, it teaches repair: a place to come back to after failure without being quietly exiled. Repair survives last of all, because it is the one wage the machine can never earn — it takes someone who can be sorry.

That is not a syllabus. It is the moral economy, taught on purpose — the meander, put back by hand.

And it is physics before policy — the line I keep ending on. Policy can build floors. It cannot manufacture belonging by decree. You build the conditions and the trust grows, because the conditions for it returned. We build the field; the field does the rest. And we build it the way you rebuild a watershed — not as one great canal, which is only the next enclosure, but as an archipelago of small commons: a network that holds your standing instead of harvesting it, a church basement running the cold-start for the people the system leaves outside, a town that decides the straight channel wasn't the only option. The first independence dissolved a political band that had become domination; the next has to restore the human bands that extraction dissolved. Not everywhere, not all at once. Here, and here, and here.

---

## In lieu of flowers

Every obituary ends the same two ways: what to do, and where to gather.

In lieu of flowers, the family asks one thing. Be useful to a single person who cannot pay you back.

And the services will be held where they have always been held — wherever people still gather to do for one another what nobody is paying for. At the ditch-bank in the spring, shovels in hand, the water not moving until everyone shows up. And out past it, on the open water, where the first people to cross open ocean did it with no written map and no modern instrument, reading the swell against the hull and the stars coming up at dusk. I have written about [sailing by starlight](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/sailing-by-starlight-d4cedff64ddf) and about [the voyage](https://medium.com/@chrisperkins505/the-voyage-12-days-of-digital-physics-724ca6f63b44) because it is the truest picture I have of what comes next. The wayfinder does not trust the map someone handed him. He trusts his nearness to the living thing — the ocean he is in, the sky no one has ever managed to fence.

That is the whole pedagogy of trust in one image. The map is someone else's authority, frozen, dead. The stars are the commons no candlemaker could ever block. There is no map of the country on the far side of the job, and there will not be one. In the country's two-hundred-fiftieth year, the word is independence. But the sentence that carried it was pledge. That is still the work: not to be free from one another, but to become free enough to be bound without being owned.

You do not get the map. You get the going. It grows as it goes.

So put down the map it handed you, and the small bright one that replaced it.

And look up.
