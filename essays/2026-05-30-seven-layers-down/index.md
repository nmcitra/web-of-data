Seven Layers Down
On the Lower Layers of Opportunity, and the Education of a Signal

The OSI model has seven layers: Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application.

The thing you are reading right now arrived through all of them.

Most people only ever see Layer 7 — the Application layer. The interface. The feed. The browser. The thing you touch. That is by design. The lo**we**r layers are supposed to disappear into usefulness. They just work.

But we have started confusing Layer 7 with the whole stack.

We tell young people to compete for the visible layer: the job that shows up on LinkedIn, the credential that gets you in the room, the Application-layer position in an Application-layer economy. And we are doing this at exactly the moment when Layer 7 is becoming the most crowded and unstable layer in the stack.

My niece Nevaeh gradudated this May. My nephew Trevious graduates shortly after. They are from Albuquerque, New Mexico — three hours north of where I live now in Las Cruces, where I have been long enough to call it home alongside the city I grew up in.

I want to tell them something nobody told me. Something I had to learn by accident, in a classroom I almost did not find, in a city I almost left.

Read the lower layers.

## Layer 7: Application

Layer 7 is where everything visible happens. The browser, the app, the interface between you and everything the network knows. The Application layer is the reason the rest of the stack exists — it is the surface, the point of contact, the place where people actually are.

It is also where the compression is happening.

Artificial intelligence is reshaping entry-level work, and the reshaping is concentrated here at Layer 7: the jobs that sit closest to the interface, the positions that have historically served as the first rung. Content work. Data annotation. Certain coding tasks. Customer service escalation. Basic research and drafting. The roles that used to teach young people how a field operates from the inside are becoming harder to find, lower-paying when found, and first in line when organizations cut.

This is not happening uniformly. The trades are not experiencing it the same way. Defense and national security work is not. Healthcare direct care is not. Film production physical roles are not. The compression is concentrated in the visible layer — which is exactly the layer that career advice points graduates toward.

Everyone is crowding Layer 7. Layer 7 is becoming a harder place to start.

This is not a reason for despair. It is a reason to read the diagram differently.

## Layer 6: Presentation

The Presentation layer translates. It takes data from the Application layer above it and encodes it for transmission — and on the receiving end, it decodes what arrives into something the Application layer can use. Without Presentation, the data might be there but unreadable. The format is wrong. The encryption key is missing. The message arrives as noise.

Career advice is a Presentation layer problem.

The encoding happens early — in guidance counselor offices, in the way college is framed, in the cultural story about what a good outcome looks like. The data gets encoded in a particular format: degree, credential, corporate position, upward trajectory. That encoding made reasonable sense for the economy it was built to describe. It is running on an outdated format for the one we are actually in.

Most graduates receive the decoded message and take it at face value. The advice sounds authoritative because the format is familiar. It rarely occurs to anyone to ask whether the translation is accurate.

My niece Nevaeh wants to be an ASL interpreter. She wants to sit between two systems that cannot communicate with each other — between a hearing world and a Deaf world — and move meaning across the gap. She wants to take what one person is saying and render it legible to another person who would otherwise receive nothing.

She wants to do Presentation layer work. She is pointing herself at exactly the right layer.

She does not have that word for it yet.

The path to ASL interpretation runs through community college programs, university training, and licensure. It runs through the specific places in New Mexico where certified interpreters are genuinely short — schools, hospitals, courtrooms, state agencies. The path exists. It has a next step. And it is the kind of work that does not disappear when the Application layer gets compressed. Translation is the thing that matters most precisely when the stakes are highest. Human Presentation layer work — reading the room, reading the body, holding the frame for both sides — is durable in ways that Application-layer entry work is not.

Nevaeh is going to be fine. She is already reading the diagram, even if she does not know it.

## Layer 5: Session

The Session layer manages connections. Before data can flow between two systems, a session has to open. The three-way handshake: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. One system initiates, the other responds, the first confirms. Without that sequence, nothing moves. The connection does not establish.

A vocation works the same way. There is a moment — sometimes sudden, sometimes slow — when a session opens between a person and a kind of work. Before that moment, everything is abstract. The diagram is just a diagram. After the session opens, the abstract becomes operational. You know what layer you are on. You know what you are carrying.

My session opened in a classroom at a community college in Albuquerque. One of my instructors was Bob Hennigan.

The Cisco Networking Academy curriculum came in five books across four terms — a full calendar year plus a few months, because you could not take them all at once. The protocol did not allow it. There was a fifth class, TSHOOT, which you took last, once you had built the whole stack in your mind and needed to learn how to find what was broken inside it.

I was twenty-two. The reasons I was not in that classroom earlier — the years between finishing eighth grade and finding my way into Bob Hennigan’s course — those reasons are mine and they stay with me. One thing I will say: the gap was real, and I crossed it.

And today, we cross another.

Bob taught the layers the way they deserved to be taught — not as memorization but as logic. Why does the Physical layer have to exist before the Data Link layer can function? Because you cannot frame data that has not been transmitted. Why does the Session layer need to open before Transport can work? Because you cannot guarantee delivery without knowing there is a connection to deliver to. The sequence was not arbitrary. It was architecture.

I passed the CCNA on the first try.

Passing the exam felt good. Making my grandparents proud was everything.

## Layer 4: Transport

The Transport layer handles end-to-end communication. Two protocols: TCP, which is connection-oriented — it establishes before it transmits, confirms delivery, retransmits what drops. And UDP, which is connectionless — it fires without waiting for confirmation. TCP is slow and reliable. UDP is fast and uncertain. (I see you multi-cast people out there!)

Most career advice recommends TCP: the credential sequence, the known path, guaranteed delivery. There is nothing wrong with TCP. TCP keeps its promise — it establishes, it confirms, it retransmits what drops. The problem is not the protocol. The problem is the address. A lot of people get routed, reliably, to destinations that were decommissioned years ago. The credential still arrives. The path still completes. There is no job at the other end — and nobody updated the routing table to say so.

A lot of people end up on UDP paths not by choice but by circumstance. Packets arrive out of order. Some do not arrive. You figure out what you received and work with it.

I was on UDP for years before I found Bob Hennigan’s classroom and enrolled in something that actually went somewhere.

Trevious does not have a direction yet. He is the open packet — headers assembled, destination address: blank. That is not a failure. That is a starting condition. The session opens when something responds to his SYN. It cannot be forced. But it is more likely when you know where to send the signal — and when you know the routing table has more in it than you have been shown.

## Layer 3: Network

The Network layer handles routing. It determines how a packet moves from origin to destination across multiple interconnected networks. It reads the destination address and decides: this packet goes that way.

The routing table is the heart of Layer 3. The map of what is reachable from here, at what cost, along which path.

When people tell a young person from Albuquerque what to do next, they are usually reading from an outdated routing table. Move to Austin. Move to Denver. Move to wherever the visible economy is loudest. The assumption embedded in that advice is that Albuquerque is a pass-through node — a place you leave, not a destination.

The actual routing table looks different. And many of the strongest entries in it share something: they are infrastructure work. They require presence. They run from the lower layers of the stack, not the Application layer where the visible economy crowds.

Sandia National Laboratories sits in the Albuquerque metro — one of the premier technical employers in the country, anchored in defense, nuclear, cybersecurity, and energy systems. Work adjacent to that ecosystem can build a security clearance, technical experience, and a portable professional signal that follows you wherever you eventually go. Kirtland Air Force Base is next door. Intel runs a fabrication plant in Rio Rancho, just outside the city, at the precise moment domestic semiconductor manufacturing has become a federal priority. Meta’s data center in Los Lunas.

New Mexico has spent a decade building film and television production infrastructure. These productions need physical crew — lighting, rigging, grip, coordination, set work — who cannot do their jobs remotely. The trades that support production are local, skilled, and underserved.

And New Mexico has more solar irradiance than almost anywhere in the country. The energy transition is a physical-layer buildout. It needs people who can work outside, install and maintain systems, and understand the layers beneath the application of clean power. That work is here. It is growing.

The packets are moving through Albuquerque. The routing table is real. Most graduates never see it because nobody taught them how to read it.

## Layer 2: Data Link

The Data Link layer handles communication between directly adjacent nodes. Not across the full network — just the next hop. The unit of transmission here is the frame: a structured package of data with a source address, a destination address, and error-checking information.

Nevaeh is graduating this May. Trevious graduates in another year. They are directly connected to me. Adjacent nodes.

I am not a guidance counselor. I am a person who grew up in Albuquerque, who wanted to leave, who found the Cisco Networking Academy instead, who ended up three hours south in Las Cruces and counts it all as home. What I have is the routing table. What I have is the diagram that Bob Hennigan put on a whiteboard in a community college classroom more than twenty years ago.

What I want to frame for them at the Data Link layer — directly, node to node — is this: the lower layers of the stack are more available and more valuable than anything they have been told to compete for at Layer 7.

For Nevaeh: the path to ASL interpretation is real. It has a next step. Take it.

For Trevious: the open packet is not a problem. It is a starting condition. The routing table exists. The sessions are available. Something will respond to your SYN if you send it somewhere real.

The frame I am offering: the stack goes deeper than what is visible. That is not a consolation. That is a structural fact about where value lives.

## Layer 1: Physical

The Physical layer is everything that is actually physical. The cable. The wire. The signal moving through it. The voltage differential that encodes a one from a zero. Nothing above it exists without it. It is the foundation — not the most complex layer, but the most fundamental. Remove it and the whole stack collapses.

It does not get talked about much. It is supposed to just work.

I learned the OSI model from a book. Five books, actually — the full Cisco Networking Academy curriculum, spread across four terms, with Bob at the front of the room. I was twenty-two. I was working the door at a bar on Central Avenue in downtown Albuquerque. The Anodyne.

My post was at the top of the stairs. During slow times I sat on an old chair that someone found on the side of the road and read. The book fell open to the same pages over and over — the layer diagrams, the protocol charts, the explanations of why each layer had to exist before the one above it could function.

At the bottom of the stairs, outside, was Mickey sitting on a chrome-legged barstool with a black round seat — though Mickey was not his name. It was what people called him because of the Mickey Mouse glove he wore religiously. A Black man, late fifties or early sixties, round face, big eyes. His clothes were not ragged. But his face looked tired some days. He did not have a home (or at least I didn’t think so). He was low-key begging in the way that people do when they have found a corner and learned how to hold it — a wave, a smile, a presence that made people feel welcomed before they walked in. He waved one glove at everyone who came through. Never demanding anything. Just there. Everyday.

I sat at the top of the stairs learning the theory of the Physical layer while Mickey worked the Physical layer outside on Central Avenue, which is Route 66, which is the original American routing infrastructure, which runs straight through the heart of Albuquerque.

He was generating something real — warmth, recognition, the feeling of being seen before you stepped into the noise above. The bar captured the value. Mickey asked for what he could get.

I thought about Mickey for years after I stopped working that job. I still think about him. Not because the story has a clean resolution — it does not, or none that I know — but because of what he showed me about Layer 1 without either of us knowing that is what was happening.

The Physical layer does not get credit. It just carries. It just runs. Everything above it depends on it and very little flows back down.

What separated my chair from Mickey’s barstool was not character. It was not effort. It was a thread — Bob Hennigan’s classroom, five books, two years of terms, an exam I passed on the first try, and a referral to a woman named Rebecca.

Passing the CCNA felt good. Making my grandparents proud was everything.

The thing you are reading is Layer 7.

I did not write it from here.

It came from Central Avenue. From Bob Hennigan’s classroom. From a stinky chair that fell out of a truck and a Cisco book. From my grandparents’ faces. From Mickey, who didn’t talk much, at the bottom of the stairs waving a white-gloved hand at strangers who were about to spend eight dollars on a drink and would never know his name.

That is the signal.

This is what it became.

And I should be honest about what just happened.

I took Mickey up the stack. His bar stool, his glove, his face — I carried them to Layer 7 and put my name under them. He generated something real on Central Avenue. The bar captured it then. I am capturing it now.

I do not get to repair that. I lost the thread to him and Angelo years ago, the same kind of thread that pulled me off the street into Bob’s classroom. What I can do is refuse to stand outside the pattern while I name it. So I am naming it. The debt does not clear.

Nevaeh. Trevious.

Do not confuse the surface with the source.

The stack is seven layers deep. The signal starts at the bottom.

Find the thread. Send the SYN.

All layers are available to you.

> In memory of “Mickey.”
