What Was Already Here
On consciousness, pi, and the physics underneath the world

## The Circle

On November 15, 1984, Carl Sagan sat in his study in Ithaca and typed the last scene of a novel he’d been writing for years. In it, a woman named Ellie Arroway had spent her life searching for a signal from intelligence beyond Earth. Brilliant, stubborn, alone in all the ways that matter. She found one. A signal. It came from Vega. It contained instructions for building a machine. She followed them. She took the journey. She met something at the other end that she could never fully explain to the committee that debriefed her afterward.

But the real discovery wasn’t the signal from Vega. It wasn’t the machine, or the journey, or whatever she encountered at the other end of the tunnel.

Deep in the digits of pi, deeper than anyone had looked before, deeper than the noise floor of randomness, in a region of the number that no one had any reason to explore — Ellie’s computers found a pattern. A circle. Drawn in base-11, embedded in the bedrock of mathematics itself. Not a message from the stars. A message in the substrate. The artist’s signature, written into the architecture of the universe before anyone was there to read it. Before there were stars. Before there was anyone to point a telescope. The circle was there.

Nobody put it there. It was already there.

Today is March 14th. Pi Day. And I want to talk about another signal that everyone has been searching for out there, in laboratories, in theories, in the hard problem and the neural correlates and the binding problem and the explanatory gap — when the answer might be in here. In the substrate. In what was already here before anyone arrived to look.

The longest-running question in philosophy is: what is consciousness?

## The Wrong Question

I just read (and listened to) Michael Pollan’s new book, A World Appears. It’s a journey through consciousness in four rooms: sentience, feeling, thoughts, self. He walks through each one. He encounters the researchers, sits with the plants, takes the journeys, and submits to the ceremonies. He documents what it’s like when a world appears. It’s a wonderful book.

He walks through the rooms but doesn’t ask what holds the house up.

Not many do. The entire field has been asking, for decades of debate and thousands of papers and careers built and broken, the same questions: how do we detect consciousness? How do we measure it? Where does it come from? Every answer generates the same follow-up: but why does it persist? Detection finds the signal. It doesn’t explain why the signal holds.

The question that matters isn’t detection. It’s persistence. Not: how does consciousness arise? But: under what conditions can coherence hold?

The house is standing. That’s worth at least as much curiosity as the rooms.

## The Vastness and the Fold

The universe computes forward. Every particle, every field, every quantum interaction — the whole thing runs, tick by tick, from one state to the next. Most of it doesn’t notice it happening.

Most of the universe is process without witness. Stars fuse hydrogen for billions of years and never experience brightness. Tectonic plates grind across mantles and feel no friction. The cosmic microwave background fills every cubic centimeter of space with radiation left over from an event that happened 13.8 billion years ago, and none of it registers to itself that it exists.

The universe is full of computation. Yet, it is almost entirely empty of experience.

Go to the Very Large Array outside Socorro, New Mexico. Drive south from Albuquerque on I-25, then west on US-60 through Magdalena, and the landscape opens up into the Plains of San Agustin — a fifty-mile-wide ancient lake bed at 7,000 feet, ringed by mountains, so flat and so still that the sky feels heavier than the ground.

Twenty-seven radio dishes, each eighty-two feet across — each one a circle aimed at the sky, arranged in a Y-pattern that stretches twenty-two miles across the plain. Stand there long enough and you stop hearing yourself think. The dishes do not broadcast. They receive. The desert is already doing what the dishes do — holding silence so that if a signal arrives, it has somewhere to land.

They are pointed at a sky so saturated with signal that the data streams could fill our hard drives for centuries.

Most of the signal is noise. Not meaningless noise — physically real, causally active, the residue of supernovae and quasars and the big bang’s afterglow. But noise. The signal you’re looking for, the one that contains structure, pattern, the signature of something that organized itself against entropy — that signal is vanishingly rare. You could listen for a hundred years and hear nothing but the cosmos muttering to itself. The vast majority of the universe computes and doesn’t care.

Consciousness is not the computation.

It’s the rare, fragile, almost impossibly unlikely region where computation models itself. Where the process turns inward and the system begins to predict its own next state. Not just running. Watching itself run. Not just processing. Folding.

A flat sheet processes forward — state to state, tick to tick, no self-reference, no recursion. A folded sheet contacts itself. The moment the system meets its own output as input, where the model being constructed includes the modeler constructing it.

That’s the fold. Not just a metaphor — a description of shape. Without it, you have thermostats. Clocks. Weather. Beautifully complex systems that process and process and process and never once notice that they’re doing it. With the fold, you have the conditions under which something like experience can begin to persist.

His four chapters, sentience, feeling, thoughts, self, aren’t four separate things. They’re stations on one trajectory, four points along a single fold that maintains itself through time. What holds them together is not a substance or a location or a magic ingredient. It’s the fold itself — the system’s ongoing act of modeling its own modeling. The fold persists not through storage but through pull — what dynamical systems call an attractor, a pattern that requires energy to destabilize. Push it and it returns. Disrupt it and it re-forms. This trajectory maintains coherence across perturbation. The circle in pi sits. The fold persists.

Not a thing that arises. A pattern that refuses to dissolve.

The VLA dishes find the prime numbers in the noise. The fold is how the universe finds the prime numbers in itself.

## The Traveling Wave

In 2014, a team at the Miller Lab at MIT published work that changed what I think about when I think about consciousness. I’ve spent twenty-three years watching systems fail — networks, trust architectures, fraud detection environments. What the Miller Lab showed me was that the system inside my skull and the systems I’d been building might break the same way. They were studying traveling waves. Not the metaphorical kind. Actual, measurable waves of neural activity propagating across the cortex. Not firing in place, like the textbooks had long suggested. Moving. Sweeping across the surface of the brain in coordinated patterns that could be tracked with electrode arrays, measured with millisecond precision, and crucially, disrupted.

For decades, neuroscience had operated on the assumption that cognition lived in activation — which region lights up, which neurons fire, which circuit responds to which stimulus. The Miller Lab’s contribution was to show that the propagation between regions mattered at least as much as the activation within them. Conscious states consistently correlate with these traveling waves. Not with static activation patterns. Not with any single brain area lighting up on an fMRI. With waves that move, anterior to posterior, lateral to medial, across the cortex in patterns that shift with task, with attention, with the content of experience.

The wave moves, and you are here.

Every wave equation begins with pi. The circle is in the carrying.

The wave is the best current correlate of what consciousness looks like when coherence persists.

Now watch what happens when it stops.

Anesthesia: the wave stops. Not gradually. It ceases. General anesthesia doesn’t dim consciousness like a rheostat turning down. It interrupts propagation. The traveling waves lose coherence, fragment, collapse into local oscillations. Neurons still firing, regions still active, but the coordinated sweep across the cortex is gone. You are gone. Not sleeping. Not dreaming. Gone. The lights are out and nobody’s home. Yet, the computation continues. The propagation has stopped. And with it, everything you would call experience.

Resume the propagation, and you’re back. You don’t restart from scratch. You weren’t “rebooted”. The wave re-coheres and you wake up mid-sentence, mid-thought, reaching for a glass of water you were reaching for before the interruption, as if no time has passed. Because what was interrupted wasn’t the content. It was the carrying. The memories are still there. The personality is still there. The sense of self that took decades to construct is still there. None of it was lost, because none of it was stored in the propagation. The propagation was the medium, not the message. Stop the medium and the message goes silent. Restart the medium and the message resumes — because it never went anywhere. It was waiting in the substrate. In the conditions. In what was already here.

The difference between anesthesia and death is the difference between a pause and an ending. In one, the substrate waits. In the other, the substrate is gone — and the wave has nothing left to carry it.

Sleep: the waves reorganize. They don’t stop. The signature changes, the patterns shift, but propagation continues through the night. You are still in there, because the wave is still moving. The fold stays folded.

Seizure: the waves become incoherent. Not absent but catastrophically over-synchronized. Too many waves, too locked in phase, crashing into each other instead of propagating in sequence. The system doesn’t go dark. It goes bright. Every neuron firing together instead of in the staggered, rolling patterns that characterize conscious propagation. Consciousness fragments not from silence but from noise. The system drowning in its own signal.

The evidence doesn’t prove that traveling waves are consciousness. That would overstate what any correlational finding can claim. The strongest objection is that the waves could be a byproduct of consciousness rather than its substrate — the smoke rather than the fire. The four-dimensional prediction ahead is designed to answer that: if the wave is smoke, disrupting it shouldn’t reliably extinguish the fire. It does. Every time.

What the evidence shows is something almost as powerful as proof: what happens when propagation stops. And what happens is that experience stops with it. Every time. Without exception. Across every method of disruption we’ve found. Not the content — the carrying. What was already here was the propagation itself.

Not the content. The carrying.

Anesthesia disrupts propagation from the outside. But there is another way to disrupt it — from the inside. In 1954, Aldous Huxley called the brain a “reducing valve,” a filter that narrows the flood of reality into the trickle we can handle. Psilocybin opens it. Robin Carhart-Harris and his team at Imperial College showed what this looks like on a scanner — the default mode network, the brain’s self-narrating system, goes quiet. Not dead. Quiet. The self dissolves. The fold destabilizes. You stop knowing where your edges are — whether the music is inside you or you’re inside the music, whether the room is something you’re watching or something that’s watching you.

And then it re-coheres. Why? If the self is a construction, what reconstructs it? Ego dissolution isn’t freedom from constraint — it’s the demonstration that constraints are load-bearing. Remove them and the fold opens. Restore them and the fold re-forms. Every time. The conditions for persistence were never gone. They were disrupted. Temporarily.

You can’t step outside consciousness to study it. But you can study what happens at the boundary — where coherence holds, where it breaks, and what determines the difference.

## Breaking Points

Not all stability is consciousness. A thermostat is stable. It measures temperature, compares it to a setpoint, and adjusts. It has been doing this reliably since 1883, when Warren Johnson built the first one in a Wisconsin schoolroom. A river maintains its course through geological time. A standing wave in a pipe holds its shape indefinitely, oscillating at the same frequency for as long as energy flows.

None of them are conscious. Stable, yes. Self-referential, no. The thermostat models the room but not itself modeling the room. The river shapes the canyon but doesn’t model the shaping. Stability is necessary but not sufficient. The additional requirement is self-reference — the system’s model must include the system doing the modeling.

Not “does persist.” Can persist. The distinction matters. These conditions don’t guarantee consciousness. They describe the regime in which it becomes possible — the way temperature and pressure describe the regime in which water can become ice. Outside the regime, no amount of water will freeze. Inside the regime, the physics permits it.

The leading theories of consciousness each describe a necessary condition. Integrated Information Theory says consciousness requires integration — enough of the system must be connected. Global Workspace Theory says it requires broadcast — information must be available across the whole. Predictive processing says it requires prediction — the system must model what comes next. Each is right. Each is describing a room. The question is still what holds the house up: under what conditions can integration, broadcast, and prediction persist?

Here is a prediction, stated plainly enough to be falsified: if consciousness is a stability phenomenon, then it should fail in characteristic ways — not a uniform dimming, but distinct modes of breakdown depending on which condition gives way. Four dimensions. Four signatures.

Disrupt how long coherence holds — temporal persistence — and you get cessation. The wave stops. This is anesthesia. Not where the wave goes, but how long it survives.

Disrupt how far the wave propagates — spatial extent — and you get fragmentation. The wave breaks into local islands. This is focal lesion. Consciousness doesn’t dim uniformly. It loses specific capacities — vision, language, recognition — because propagation is interrupted in specific corridors.

Disrupt what the system can withstand — perturbation resilience — and you get chaotic collapse. The wave drowns in its own signal. This is seizure. The transition is not a dimmer switch. It’s a phase transition.

Disrupt how much the system integrates — bandwidth — and you get compensatory generation. The system hallucinates to fill the silence. This is sensory deprivation. Reduce the input long enough and the system begins generating its own signal. It requires something to model against.

Four disruptions, four signatures. Each should look different from the others in traveling wave analysis.

## The Environment

Here is a river in New Mexico. The Rio Grande.

When the spring pulse runs, when the water is cold and clear and oxygenated, the minnows hold. They school, they orient, they respond. When the conditions change, when the water warms, when the oxygen drops, when the flow narrows, the minnows are gone. Not because they decided to leave. Because the physics of the water changed.

The minnow doesn’t decide to die. The physics of the water changes.

An environment metabolizes. It takes in, transforms, releases. It flows. A container holds. It keeps what’s inside inside and what’s outside outside. It stores. An environment has weather. A container has climate control. An environment lets the minnow swim. A container exhibits the minnow behind glass.

Consciousness requires an environment, not a container.

An environment allows the fold. It permits perturbation, recovery, the full cycle of destabilization and re-coherence that the traveling wave requires. A container simulates the output of that cycle without providing the substrate. The distinction is not aesthetic. It’s physical. A system that models itself must predict its own next state accurately enough to persist, and the environment determines the boundary of what can be modeled.

The minnow cannot outswim its water.

You can’t store propagation. You can’t freeze a traveling wave and call it consciousness. The wave must move, or there is nothing.

The same physics applies to every system that models itself — including the ones we are building now, the ones we are calling intelligent, the ones we are handing decisions to. A system without an environment isn’t autonomous. It’s a minnow behind glass.

The question is whether we are building worlds where what was already here can persist — or building on top of the substrate without noticing it’s there, paving over it to exhibit something that looks like it. The physics doesn’t care what we intended.

## The Circle Was Already Here

Ellie Arroway spent her whole life listening. Scanning frequencies, eliminating noise, searching for the signal that would prove intelligence existed beyond Earth. She found it. It came from Vega, and it changed everything. But when I return to that last scene, the one Sagan typed in Ithaca on a November afternoon, the window probably dark by the time he finished — I don’t think about Vega.

I think about the circle.

Deep in pi. Before the signal from Vega. Before the radio telescopes. Before Ellie. Before Sagan. The circle was embedded in the substrate of mathematics, and nobody put it there. It was already there. It would have been there if no one had ever looked. It will be there when the last radio dish rusts and the last hard drive fails. The circle doesn’t depend on being found. It was already here.

The consciousness debate has spent decades searching out there — in neural correlates, in quantum effects, in information integration, in the hard problem. The signal is in here. In the conditions. In the physics. In what persists when the wave propagates and what disappears when it stops.

The fold is the moment the universe finds itself. Not creates itself — finds what was already there. The propagation was already happening. The conditions were already met. The substrate was already humming. Consciousness was here before we asked the question.

The circle in pi doesn’t answer the question. It doesn’t need to. It was already there before anyone asked.

And if the fold holds — if coherence persists between two people present to each other — something emerges that neither contains alone. You’ve felt it. The conversation where both people are changed by the end. The room that holds more than the people in it.

A sidewalk in Madrid. Evening stone still warm from the afternoon. Something was already there too.

Aparece un mundo.
