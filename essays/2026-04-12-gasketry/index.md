Gasketry


On the seams between systems and the people they're supposed to serve

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I'm writing this somewhere between Dallas Love Field and DCA, the last leg of two flights out of El Paso — which is how you get anywhere from Las Cruces, New Mexico.

I'm headed to Splunk's GovSummit to host a breakout session on Wednesday afternoon. [AI-Driven Public Sector Defense and Fraud Prevention](https://www.splunkgovsummit.com/govsummit2026/speakers) — it's about the new threat surface that opens when adversarial actors who already understand your systems better than you do wield machine-speed tools against them. That's my day job. Two decades of watching what happens when the technology outruns the institution's ability to absorb it.

But the discussion unfolding between three people I have immense respect for on my phone is more interesting than my latest addiction, Crossplay aka Scrabble. I digress.

Erie Meyer published a [piece](https://fedscoop.com/when-philanthropy-mandates-ai-solutions-taxpayers-pay-the-price/) Friday that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. She said what a lot of us have been thinking: the philanthropic funders backing civic tech don't care what the problem is, as long as the answer is AI. They're writing checks with one condition. Use our technology. Have a simple fix? A cheap test? Want to just listen to the veterans? You can't even apply for the grant unless you cram in AI.

On Saturday, Mikey Dickerson amplified it and added the part that only someone who's been inside the machine would say: if current reform efforts are funded by AI companies and show up having pre-decided that the solution is more AI, that doesn't sound very different from the Oracle contracts we spent a decade trying to escape. [Check out Mikey's piece here.](https://medium.com/@mikeyd/clearing-the-ai-r-287ea3930bd6)

On Sunday, Jen Pahlka [responded](https://www.eatingpolicy.com/p/yes-philanthropy-should-fund-ai-in). She doesn't disagree with Erie as much as it looks. She says AI might be the tool that finally lets us map the regulatory tangle — the 7,119 pages of active UI regulations that New Jersey's labor commissioner literally stacked in boxes and carried into a hearing room. Stanford's RegLab can read an entire regulatory regime in an afternoon and return a map of the mess. That's not techno-solutionism. That's cartography.

I'm not here to argue with any of them. I'm here to tell them about the dirt!

### Four Clocks

Here's what I've learned after two decades in network defense, trust architecture, and anti-fraud: every system failure I've ever dug into was a clock mismatch.

There are four clocks in any system that involves humans and technology. They run at different speeds. And the failures are felt and happen at the margins and seams.

Biological clock. Trust accumulates here. Our nervous systems decide whether to extend trust in milliseconds, drawing on years of pattern recognition we can't quite articulate. The handshake. The eye contact. The thing your gut knows before your brain catches up.

- This clock runs at the speed of the body.

Human clock. Relationships develop here. The neighbor you've borrowed tools from for a decade. The colleague you've watched handle three crises. The veteran who just needs to know the form went through.

- This clock runs at the speed of a life.

Institutional clock. Policy, regulation, governance. The 7,119 pages. The procurement cycle. The legislative session.

- This clock runs at the speed of bureaucracy.

Machine clock. Computation. The thing generating one million full-fledged identities in the matter of minutes and the thing that can read 7,119 pages in four minutes and process a million claims before lunch.

- This clock runs at the speed of light, electrons, and silicon. ⚡️

Every person in this debate is right. They're just talking about different clocks.

### The Gasket

Erie's submit button fix at the VA is the clearest example I know of a clock gasket done right.

Veterans were mashing the submit button because they couldn't tell the form had gone through. One line of code. Machine-speed fix. But the fix worked because it served the human clock — the veteran's real-time experience of uncertainty. The machine clock didn't impose itself or tell the user what time it was. It resolved something the human clock was struggling with. The gasket between the two holds with this maintenance.

That fix wasn't cheap because it was simple. It was right because it changed the environment the veteran was interacting with. The form stopped producing duplicate submissions. The backlog shrank. The thing the veteran touched every day got better. Environment changed. Problem solved.

An AI chatbot that "helps" veterans tell time and navigate the same broken form is a different kind of intervention. The machine clock is serving itself. The veteran still faces the broken environment. The form is still confusing. The process is still opaque. The AI just helps the veteran move faster through a system that shouldn't exist in its current shape. That's not a gasket. That's a lubricant applied to a machine that needs rebuilding.

Fix the thing the person touches. Change the environment.

### The Map and the Terrain

Jen's regulatory mapping vision is the strongest version of the AI argument, and she's right about it. Machine clock reading 7,119 pages and returning a map of the tangle... that's AI doing what AI does.

Cartography. Survey. Measurement. Gasketry.

The machine measures the terrain at machine speed so the humans can decide what to change at institutional speed. This is the whole point.

But here's where the clocks diverge. The map arrives in four minutes. The simplification takes four years. The AI produces the map immediately, at machine speed. The terrain changes at institutional speed. If nobody builds the gasket between those two clocks, the map sits in a drawer while the tangle compounds.

And here's the thing the map won't tell you by itself: some of those 7,119 pages aren't just complex. They're captured. Corruption is the capture of the legal and regulatory framework. Some of that tangle exists because someone needed it to exist — because a vendor needed a procurement rule, because an industry needed an exemption, because complexity is where extraction hides. AI can map the tangle. It cannot tell you which knots are there by accident and which are there by design. That takes a human who's been in the dirt long enough to know the difference.

A signal with no cost carries no information. AI hype is a loud signal. But who's paying for it?

The people paying are my wife's grandparents and the civic tech teams forced to cram AI into proposals where a one-line code fix would do, and the government employees sitting through mandatory Gemini training when they need help with their actual workflow.

The cost falls on the people with the least power to refuse it.

### The Trust Threshold

Mikey's Oracle comparison is about what happens when you skip gasketry entirely. You bolt machine-speed technology onto institutional-speed procurement. The institution can't absorb it at its clock speed. The technology calcifies (aka tech debt). The vendor entrenches. The institution loses the ability to change because the machine-speed system has locked-in the topology. Same physics. Different vendor. Same failure.

The Internet mandate worked in 2012 because the technology had been proving itself for a decade. Amazon was obliterating anyone who didn't adapt. People were genuinely abandoning services that didn't have a web presence. That expectation was backed by behavior — not just taste and preference, but action. People voted with their time, their money, their attention. The signal was costly. It carried real information about what the world needed.

AI in 2026 isn't there yet. My customer service bot/agent experiences have been bad at best. Yours probably have too. Nobody is abandoning government services because they can't chat with an AI agent instead of a human last I checked. The expectation exists as aspiration, not as the kind of costly signal that justified the Internet mandate. When the signal becomes costly — when people are genuinely choosing AI-mediated services over traditional ones because the difference in quality is undeniable — then the mandate will be justified by the same physics that justified the Internet mandate. But not before.

+1 to Mikey's: "I don't think those expectations are there yet for AI."

### The Ditch

I grew up with acequias in New Mexico — gravity-fed irrigation systems that have been carrying water since 800 AD. I live on the foothills, just a few miles from the nearest ditch, and I've never lived far from them.

Every spring, the parciantes clean the ditch. La limpia. They show up with shovels. They work alongside each other. The mayordomo walks the ditch with them, not above them. When it's done, everyone knows who showed up. Everyone can attest to who did the work. The ditch flows. The community holds.

No one mandated the technology. No one wrote a grant requiring shovels. The system works because it runs on all four clocks simultaneously, and the gaskets between them are built into the practice itself.

The water flows at physical speed — gravity, grade, season. That's the substrate.

The parciantes show up at biological speed — annual, embodied, their backs and hands in the mud. That's where trust accumulates.

The community governs at institutional speed — the mayordomo, the commission, the vote on water rights. That's where the rules live.

And the measurement, who showed up, who contributed, who can attest, operates at human speed. That's the record. Or, Gasketry if you're still with me.

Four clocks. All present. The gaskets between them are the reason it's lasted twelve centuries.

### What I'd Tell the Funders

If I had five minutes with the people writing the AI-or-bust grant conditions, I wouldn't say "don't fund AI." I wouldn't say "AI is the answer." I'd say: tell me which clock you're trying to serve.

If you're serving the machine clock — faster processing, better pattern matching, automated translation — then AI is probably the right tool. U.S. Digital Response's translation work in New Jersey is a perfect example. Machine clock serving human clock. The gasket held. More people applied for benefits because they could finally read the form. That's real.

If you're serving the institutional clock — regulatory simplification, policy redesign, procurement reform — then AI is a survey instrument, not a solution. Fund the map. But also fund the people who will use the map to change the terrain. A map without the institutional will to act on it is decoration. The original will be mounted in a fancy building and someone will make prints. They might even say "limited edition."

If you're serving the human clock — the veteran who can't tell if the form went through, the nurse navigating a contractor app that prices her desperation — then ask whether the intervention changes the environment or just helps the person navigate a fucked up one. Erie's submit button fix changed the environment. An AI chatbot navigates the broken one. The first one lasts. The second one maintains the conditions that made it necessary.

If you're serving the biological clock — the deep trust that accumulates only through presence, reciprocity, and time — then no technology serves it. Technology can measure it. Technology can verify it. Technology cannot substitute for it. The la limpia works because people show up with their bodies and work alongside each other and are witnessed doing it. No app replaces that. The trust that holds a community together accumulates at biological speed in each of us, and there is no machine-speed or legislative shortcut.

### The Terrain

I will try to extend my stay in DC to make Mikey's book launch on Friday (I can't wait to read your book!). Family time in Albuquerque next weekend, so the window is tight. But I'll make every effort.

I want to show up because that's how this works, right? You read someone's book and it changes how you think. You read an op-ed and it articulates something you've been feeling for a decade at the fraud desk. You read a response and it challenges you to be more precise. And then you show up. In person. At human speed. Because the trust that matters accumulates in the room, not in the tangle.

From my vantage point, here in the New Mexican dirt, the debate about AI in government is a debate about maps. Who has the better map. Which map to use. Which technology to mandate. Which funder to please. All of that matters. None of it is the terrain.

The terrain is the trust network underneath the technology. The maintenance functions that keep it above the threshold where a community holds. The four clocks and the gaskets between them. The ditch that's been carrying water, maintained by people who show up every spring with shovels and do the work alongside each other.

AI is the best cartography tool we've ever built. I believe that. I'm giving a talk about it on Wednesday. It maps faster, measures more, and sees patterns no human team can find. But cartography is not terrain. The map is not the dirt. And the dirt has a structure that no amount of mapping will change unless the mapmaker walks the ditch.

And then, right after that, we'll be wayfinding.

So here's my question for the funders, the reformers, the practitioners, and everyone arguing about which technology to deploy:

What are you maintaining?
